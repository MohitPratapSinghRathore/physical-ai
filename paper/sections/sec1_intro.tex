\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A government that guarantees mortgages, lends for education and funds itself from payroll and
income taxes has taken a position on wages without ever describing it as one. If income moves
from labor to capital, that position loses value on both sides at once: the receipts that service
the debt fall, and the credit the government has guaranteed deteriorates for the same reason.
Whether the government holds an offsetting claim on the capital that replaced the labor is an
empirical question, and it is the question this paper examines.

\paragraph{The central proposition.} Wage-dependent public revenues and credit guarantees
concentrate automation exposure on the federal balance sheet, while household and lender outcomes
vary with income, debt composition and the mechanism of displacement. The measurements that
support it are these. About \result{DebtOnlyDirect} percent of all US debt, public and private, is
serviced directly from wages, and \result{DebtOnlyIndirect} percent once indirect servicing is
counted. Counting the federal government as exposed when it is holder, guarantor or debtor, it is
exposed on about \result{SovereignUnion} percent of the directly wage-backed claims.
Section~\ref{sec:accounts} sets that share beside the federal share of the separately measured
claims on AI capital, as a contrast in composition between two differently measured pools.

Whether the state holds anything against that exposure is a common-dollar, common-period
question, and Section~\ref{sec:fiscal} is where the paper examines it: what the tax system
actually collects from the income that replaces wages, and what replacement would require. That
answer has limits, and they are stated there.

\paragraph{What the engine is, and is not.} The displacement results in this paper come from a
conditional labor-income-loss stress test. It asks what happens to balance sheets and to the
budget if a given share of the wage bill disappears. It is not a model of artificial intelligence,
not a forecast of how much labor AI will displace, and not a causal estimate of anything. The
displacement level is an input we vary, not an output we predict, and the results are
scenario-based balance-sheet arithmetic conditional on that input. One level, ten percent of the
wage bill, is the calibrated scenario with the least extrapolation, in the sense that the
reemployment relationship stays inside its observed range there; every figure above it is reported
as a band.

\paragraph{Structure.} The accounts and the holder map are the paper's contribution; everything
else supports them. Section~\ref{sec:accounts} builds labor backing accounts and reports the
asymmetry they reveal. Three supporting modules follow: the capital tax decomposition of
Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}, the conditional stress test of Sections~\ref{sec:displacement}
and~\ref{sec:banks}, and the AI-bust case of Section~\ref{sec:bust}, which prices the opposite
direction on the same basis. Section~\ref{sec:institutions} draws the institutional implications
and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the limitations. Each module opens by saying which part
of the central claim it supports.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes four.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item \textbf{A measurement framework.} Labor backing accounts decompose the stock of US financial
claims two ways at once, by servicing income and by holder. The framework yields a ratio, a
federal exposure share, a holder map for both sides, a series from 1952, and a breakdown by wage
quintile, with the conventions and their sensitivities stated in full.

\item \textbf{A holder map of both sides.} The wage side and the AI side are separately measured
objects and are reported side by side rather than netted against each other. The AI side is built
from company filings, so it is reported as bounds: a measured lower bound on the level, and
therefore an upper bound on the federal share of it.

\item \textbf{A decomposition of the capital tax that would have to replace the wage tax.} We
rebuild the effective marginal rate on income that shifts from wages to AI capital from
components, taking the treatment of expensing from \citet{AcemogluManeraRestrepo2020}, and find
that the binding constraint is the ownership of corporate claims rather than offshore profit
shifting.

\item \textbf{A conditional incidence result.} In the calibrated scenario with the least
extrapolation we measure where the first-round loss lands across the federal government, households, the housing
agencies and \result{NInstitutions} bank and credit union balance sheets, and test whether
household relief protects bank capital.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.} We do not forecast automation, and we do not claim the fiscal
mechanism, which is established in the work reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related}. We do not claim
that the framework is new in the strong sense: our search located no prior construction of the
same object, but that search was not systematic, so the claim is ``none located'' rather than
``none exists''. And we do not claim that the capital tax result settles which tax base is the
right one; we argue for a marginal, flow-based rate and state the case against it in
Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}.
