\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A government that guarantees mortgages, lends for education and funds itself from payroll and
income taxes has taken a position on wages without ever describing it as one. If income moves
from labor to capital, that position loses value on both sides at once: the receipts that service
the debt fall, and the credit the government has guaranteed deteriorates for the same reason.
Whether the government holds an offsetting claim on the capital that replaced the labor is an
empirical question, and it is the question this paper answers.

\paragraph{The central claim.} The United States financial system carries a large stock of claims
whose servicing depends on labor income. The public sector holds, guarantees or owes most of
them, and has little direct stake in the capital associated with an artificial intelligence
transition. Under conditional wage-displacement scenarios, the first-round burden falls mainly on
the public sector and on households with thin liquid buffers, rather than on aggregate bank
capital. Three measurements support that claim. About \result{DebtOnlyDirect} percent of all US
debt, public and private, is serviced directly from wages, and \result{DebtOnlyIndirect} percent
once indirect servicing is counted. The federal government is exposed, as holder, guarantor or
debtor, on about \result{SovereignUnion} percent of the directly wage-backed claims. On the other
side, the claims on AI capital that we can observe put the federal share at about
\result{AIFederalShareRounded} percent, and that side is a measured lower bound rather than a
complete count.

\paragraph{What the engine is, and is not.} The displacement results in this paper come from a
conditional labor-income-loss stress test. It asks what happens to balance sheets and to the
budget if a given share of the wage bill disappears. It is not a model of artificial intelligence,
not a forecast of how much labor AI will displace, and not a causal estimate of anything. The
displacement level is an input we vary, not an output we predict, and the results are
scenario-based balance-sheet arithmetic conditional on that input. Only one level, ten percent of
the wage bill, sits inside the range of the data used to calibrate reemployment; every figure
above that is reported as a band and labeled as outside the data.

\paragraph{Structure.} The accounts and the holder map are the paper's contribution; everything
else supports them. Section~\ref{sec:accounts} builds labor backing accounts, which decompose the
stock of financial claims by the income that services each claim and by who holds, guarantees or
owes it, and reports the asymmetry between the two sides. Three supporting modules follow. The
capital tax decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:fiscal} asks whether the tax that reaches AI capital
could replace the wage taxes lost. The conditional stress test in
Sections~\ref{sec:displacement} and~\ref{sec:banks} asks where a given loss of wage income lands,
first by holder and then across individual bank and credit union balance sheets. The AI-bust case
in Section~\ref{sec:bust} prices the opposite direction on the same basis.
Section~\ref{sec:institutions} draws the institutional implications and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the limitations. Each module opens by saying which part of
the central claim it supports.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes four.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item \textbf{A measurement framework.} Labor backing accounts decompose the stock of US financial
claims two ways at once, by servicing income and by holder. The framework yields a ratio, a
federal exposure share, a holder map for both sides, a series from 1952, and a breakdown by wage
quintile, with the conventions and their sensitivities stated in full.

\item \textbf{A holder map that shows the asymmetry.} Both sides are measured on the same basis so
they can be compared rather than asserted. The AI side is built from on-balance-sheet filings, so
it is reported as bounds: a measured lower bound on the level, and therefore an upper bound on the
federal share of it.

\item \textbf{A decomposition of the capital tax that would have to replace the wage tax.} We
rebuild the effective marginal rate on income that shifts from wages to AI capital from
components, taking the treatment of expensing from \citet{AcemogluManeraRestrepo2020}, and find
that the binding constraint is the ownership of corporate claims rather than offshore profit
shifting.

\item \textbf{A conditional incidence result.} At the displacement level inside the observed data
we measure where the first-round loss lands across the federal government, households, the housing
agencies and \result{NInstitutions} bank and credit union balance sheets, and test whether
household relief protects bank capital.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.} We do not forecast automation, and we do not claim the fiscal
mechanism, which is established in the work reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related}. We do not claim
that the framework is new in the strong sense: our search located no prior construction of the
same object, but that search was not systematic, so the claim is ``none located'' rather than
``none exists''. And we do not claim that the capital tax result settles which tax base is the
right one; we argue for a marginal, flow-based rate and state the case against it in
Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}.
