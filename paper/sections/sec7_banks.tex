\section{Banks and the second round}
\label{sec:banks}

This module tests the last clause of the central claim, that household and lender outcomes vary
with income, debt composition and the mechanism of displacement rather than with aggregate bank
capital. Displacement reaches banks mostly through what lost spending does to everyone else, not
through the borrowers who lose their jobs. We apply the loss across \result{NInstitutions}
individual balance sheets and ask which are short of capital afterwards. The balance sheets are
measured; every loss applied to them is scenario.

\subsection{Little happens at the least-extrapolated level}

At ten percent of the wage bill displaced, system losses are about \result{SysLossTen} billion
dollars, of which \result{SysFirstTen} billion is first round and \result{SysSecondTen} billion,
\result{SecondRoundShareTen} percent of the total, arrives through the second round.
\result{BreachTenN} institutions fall below their capital threshold, holding
\result{BreachTenAssets} percent of system assets, against a baseline of
\result{BaselineBreaches} institutions and \result{BaselinePctAssets} percent before any loss.

Breaches open above that level, and how far they open depends heavily on whether a year of
earnings is allowed to absorb losses first, which is the largest single sensitivity in this
module; Appendix~\ref{app:detail} gives the counts at the larger displacement levels under both
treatments.

\subsection{Which institutions}

The result worth reporting is the dispersion, in Figure~\ref{fig:breach}. Card-heavy lenders are
the most exposed by a distance, \result{CardHeavyTwentyFiveInst} percent of them breaching at
twenty-five percent displacement and holding \result{CardHeavyTwentyFiveAssets} percent of that
class's assets, rising to \result{CardHeavyFiftyInst} percent of institutions at fifty. Credit
unions are the only class with any stress at the ten percent level,
\result{CreditUnionTenInst} percent of them, because their book is almost entirely household
credit with no commercial business to dilute it. Mortgage portfolio lenders are among the least
exposed at every level, \result{MortgageLenderTwentyFiveInst} percent of institutions at
twenty-five percent.

What makes a lender fragile to a wage shock is an unsecured consumer book, whose loss given
default is close to one, rather than a mortgage book concentrated in one region or industry. Two
qualifications travel with that: losses are allocated pro rata by category, so business mix is the
only source of dispersion here and the true spread is wider; and the largest institutions are
diversified by construction, so this is a statement about the tail rather than the center.

\subsection{What household relief does, under two treatments}

Whether relief reaches bank capital turns on one modeling choice, so we report the result under
both settings of it. Hold the second round fixed and an instrument can only reach the first, which
at the ten percent level is about a ninth of the total; let income replacement raise the share of
the displaced wage bill that households actually receive, and it also reduces the demand shortfall
that drives the second round, since that shortfall is a function of the same quantity.

With the propensities the second-round module already carries, the combined package of
forbearance, automatic income-driven student repayment and enhanced wage insurance removes
\result{ReliefFeedbackTen} percent of system losses at the ten percent level,
\result{ReliefFeedbackTwentyFive} percent at twenty-five and \result{ReliefFeedbackFifty} percent
at fifty. With the second round held fixed it removes \result{ReliefNoFeedbackTen},
\result{ReliefNoFeedbackTwentyFive} and \result{ReliefNoFeedbackFifty} percent.

These are two treatments of one modeling choice and not bounds on a single quantity, because
their horizons do not match: the instrument as costed runs for one year, while the loss path it is
set against runs for several. The fixed-second-round treatment asks what relief does if demand
never responds, and the feedback treatment asks what it does if a year of income replacement holds
the demand shortfall down over the whole path. Neither question is the one a designer needs
answered, and the gap between the two figures is mostly the horizon rather than the mechanism. A
duration-matched experiment, in which the instrument and the loss path run over the same years, is
future work, and until it is run this result carries less weight than the incidence results around
it.

The effect on the institutions that actually fail is large under both treatments, and that is the
more stable finding. At the twenty-five percent level, assets in breach fall from
\result{ReliefAssetsBeforeTwentyFive} percent to \result{ReliefAssetsNoFeedbackTwentyFive} percent
without feedback and \result{ReliefAssetsFeedbackTwentyFive} percent with it; card-heavy lenders go
from \result{CardHeavyTwentyFiveAssets} percent of their class's assets in breach to
\result{CardHeavyReliefNoFeedback} and \result{CardHeavyReliefFeedback} percent. At the fifty
percent level, credit unions go to \result{CUReliefNoFeedbackFifty} and
\result{CUReliefFeedbackFifty} percent. The fiscal cost of the package is
\result{ReliefCostTen} billion dollars at the ten percent level,
\result{ReliefCostTwentyFive} billion at twenty-five and \result{ReliefCostFifty} billion at fifty.

Two conclusions hold under both treatments. Relief reaches bank capital mainly by supporting
spending rather than by curing the defaults of displaced borrowers, so an instrument that replaces
income does more for the banking system than one that reschedules a claim. And relief protects the
institutions whose books are concentrated in household credit, which are the ones that fail
first.

\subsection{The exposures this engine does not reach}

Four sets of institutions are exposed in ways the panel does not capture, and Appendix~\ref{app:detail} names them rather than putting numbers on them the data cannot support: state and local government, exposed through its own wage-linked tax base under a balanced-budget constraint whose procyclical spending cut is not in the engine; pension funds, whose contributions fall with covered payroll while benefits do not; insurers and private credit, the holders most exposed to both sides at once; and everything outside the insured depository system, which is absent from the panel and as a class more thinly capitalized than what we measure. Each omission makes the system figures conservative in a direction we can sign but not size.
