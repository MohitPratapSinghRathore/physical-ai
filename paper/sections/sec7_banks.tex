\section{Banks and the second round}
\label{sec:banks}

Displacement reaches banks, but not mainly through the borrowers who lose their jobs. It reaches
them through what the lost spending does to everyone else. This section applies the loss across
\result{NInstitutions} individual balance sheets and asks which of them is short of capital
afterwards, and then asks whether household relief is the instrument that protects them. The
balance sheets are measured. Every loss applied above the level inside the data is scenario, and
labelled so in every table.

\subsection{Almost nothing happens at the level inside the data}

At ten percent of the wage bill displaced, system losses are about \result{SysLossTen} billion
dollars, of which \result{SysFirstTen} billion is first round and \result{SysSecondTen} billion,
\result{SecondRoundShareTen} percent of the total, arrives through the second round: the spending
shortfall, house prices and business credit. \result{BreachTenN} institutions fall below their
capital threshold, holding \result{BreachTenAssets} percent of system assets, against a baseline of
\result{BaselineBreaches} institutions and \result{BaselinePctAssets} percent before any loss is
applied. On any reading that is a banking system that barely moves.

Breaches open above the level inside the data. At twenty-five percent, \result{BreachTwentyFiveN}
institutions holding \result{BreachTwentyFiveAssets} percent of assets breach; at fifty percent,
\result{BreachFiftyN} institutions holding \result{BreachFiftyAssets} percent. Allowing one year of
earnings to absorb losses before the capital test is applied cuts those to
\result{BreachTwentyFiveAssetsEarnings} and \result{BreachFiftyAssetsEarnings} percent, which is
the single largest sensitivity in this module and the reason the upper rows should be read as
shape rather than level. Both are scenario figures outside the observed data.

\subsection{Which institutions, by size and by business model}

The interesting result is not the system total but its dispersion, in
Table~\ref{tab:instmodel}. Card-heavy lenders are the most exposed by a distance:
\result{CardHeavyTwentyFiveInst} percent of them breach at twenty-five percent displacement,
holding \result{CardHeavyTwentyFiveAssets} percent of that class's assets, rising to
\result{CardHeavyFiftyInst} percent of institutions at fifty. Credit unions are the only class with
any stress at the ten percent level, \result{CreditUnionTenInst} percent of them, because their
book is almost entirely household credit and they have no commercial business to diversify into.
At the other end, mortgage portfolio lenders are among the least exposed at every level,
\result{MortgageLenderTwentyFiveInst} percent of institutions at twenty-five percent displacement.

That last result settles at institution level a question left open elsewhere in this literature.
A mortgage book concentrated in one region or one industry is not what makes a lender fragile to
displacement; an unsecured consumer book is, because unsecured credit has no collateral to
recover against and its loss given default is close to one.

Two qualifications travel with the dispersion. Losses are allocated pro rata by category
holdings, so the only thing generating dispersion here is business mix; differences in
underwriting within a category would widen it, which makes these figures a lower bound on how
uneven the outcome is. And the largest institutions are diversified by construction, so a
concentration result of this kind says more about the tail of the system than about its centre.

\subsection{The instrument test: household relief does not protect bank capital}

If the second round is what reaches banks, then instruments that act on the first round should not
do much for bank capital. Table~\ref{tab:relief} tests that directly, running each relief
instrument through the same balance sheets.

Forbearance, automatic income-driven student repayment and enhanced wage insurance, all together,
remove \result{ReliefRemovedTen} percent of system bank losses at the ten percent level,
\result{ReliefRemovedTwentyFive} percent at twenty-five and \result{ReliefRemovedFifty} percent at
fifty. Under a tenth, everywhere. The arithmetic behind that is already in the first paragraph of
this section: at the ten percent level only \result{SysFirstTen} billion of a
\result{SysLossTen} billion total is first round, and the instruments act on the first round. The
most expensive instrument in the set, enhanced wage insurance, costs
\result{WageInsuranceCost} billion dollars and removes \result{WageInsuranceRemoved} billion of
bank losses.

The conclusion to draw is about what these instruments are for, not about whether they are worth
having.

\begin{quote}
Household relief instruments must be judged on household outcomes, not on bank capital. They do
reshape the distribution of failures: at the twenty-five percent level they take assets in breach
from \result{ReliefAssetsBeforeTwentyFive} to \result{ReliefAssetsAfterTwentyFive} percent and stop
\result{ReliefStoppedTwentyFive} institutions breaching. They change who fails, not how much is
lost.
\end{quote}

Those two facts are consistent, and the reason is worth a sentence because it is easy to read as a
contradiction. The instruments remove a small share of a large aggregate loss, but they remove it
from precisely the institutions whose books are concentrated in the relieved categories. A small
total with a concentrated incidence can change many capital outcomes while barely moving the
system total. An instrument chosen to protect capital would instead have to act on the second
round, which means acting on spending, house prices and business credit.

\subsection{The institutions the engine does not reach}

Four sets of institutions are exposed to wage risk in ways this engine does not capture, and they
are named here rather than estimated, because naming what is missing is more useful than putting a
number on it that the data cannot support.

\paragraph{State and local government.} It is exposed through its own tax base rather than a loan
book: \result{StateLocalWageLinkedBn} billion dollars of wage-linked income tax,
\result{StateLocalWageLinkedShare} percent of its own tax receipts, plus sales taxes that fall with
the spending wages fund. Unlike the federal government it cannot run the deficit through, because
every state but one has a balanced-budget requirement of some form. A revenue fall therefore
becomes an in-year spending cut, and that cut feeds the same demand channel that produces nine
tenths of the bank losses above. That loop is not in the engine. It would amplify the second round,
so its absence makes our figures conservative in a direction we can sign but not size.

\paragraph{Pension funds.} They are exposed on both sides at once and in opposite directions.
Benefit promises are fixed in nominal terms; contributions are a percentage of covered payroll and
fall with the wage bill. Displacement widens the funding gap mechanically, with no change in asset
returns, and because amortization schedules are set as a share of payroll, a smaller payroll raises
the required contribution rate on the employers who remain. For public plans that employer is the
same balanced-budget government in the paragraph above, which is how the two exposures compound.
What the accounts capture is only the \result{PensionHoldingsBn} billion dollars of wage-backed
claims pension funds hold, not what they owe against payroll.

\paragraph{Insurers and private credit.} These are the holders most directly exposed to both sides
of the bet at once, which makes them the population to which the paper's central asymmetry most
directly applies. Insurers hold \result{InsurerHoldingsBn} billion dollars of wage-backed claims
against long-dated liabilities, and can hold to maturity in a way a leveraged holder cannot. Private
credit cannot be separated from the Z.1 other-financial aggregate with the data used here, so the
\result{OtherFinancialHoldingsBn} billion dollars attributed to that sector is an upper bound on
the private credit holding by a wide and unknown margin. On the other side, large banks have
committed about \result{AIBankCommitmentsBn} billion dollars to AI-adjacent industries, which is
the point at which the two sides of the bet meet on one balance sheet.

\paragraph{Everything outside the insured depository system.} The panel covers insured banks and
credit unions. Nonbank mortgage servicers, consumer lenders and marketplace platforms hold
household credit and are not in it. Their absence is not neutral: they are, as a class, more thinly
capitalized and more dependent on wholesale funding than the institutions we measure, so the
system-wide figures here are again a lower bound.
