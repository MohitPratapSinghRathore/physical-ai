\section{If AI fails}
\label{sec:bust}

This module prices the opposite direction on the same basis, so that the asymmetry in the central
claim is not an artifact of having stress-tested only one outcome. For most of this project the
failure case was assessed against historical analogies while displacement went through an engine,
which flattered it. The answer is that an AI bust is a large fiscal event and a small credit
event, which inverts the displacement case.

\subsection{What the AI side is financed with, in bounds}

Any claim about a bust depends on how the investment was financed, so we report the AI side as
bounds by tier, never as a point. Table~\ref{tab:aitiers} gives them.

The measured lower bound is nine registrants' own filings: \result{FilerCapexBn} billion dollars
of capital spending against \result{FilerOCFBn} billion of operating cash flow, a self-funding
ratio of \result{SelfFunding}, and \result{OnBalanceSheetAIDebtBn} billion dollars of
on-balance-sheet debt, putting the debt-financed share of capital spending at
\result{DebtFinancedShare} percent on those books. A verified secondary tier adds about
\result{AIBankCommitmentsBn} billion dollars of bank commitments to AI-adjacent industries, of
which roughly \result{AIDrawnBn} billion appears drawn \citep{CohenKillenLau2026}. A scenario tier
covers the equity attributable to AI, swept rather than asserted. The fourth tier,
off-balance-sheet vehicles, hardware-backed lending and securitizations, is not sourced at all,
and \citet{BIS2026} describes exactly those structures as dominant in this market. Every level is
therefore a lower bound and every federal share of it an upper bound.

\subsection{The financing verdict, which is conditional}

Two historical patterns bracket a bust of this kind, with different fiscal consequences. In the
equity-led case of 2000 to 2002 federal receipts fell \result{EquityLedReceiptsPct} percent and
corporate tax \result{EquityLedCorpPct} percent; in the debt-led case of 2007 to 2009 receipts
fell \result{CreditLedReceiptsPct} percent and corporate tax \result{CreditLedCorpPct} percent.
Both are verified from published aggregates.

On the nine filers' own books the structure sits near the equity-led pattern. That is where a
paper could stop and it would be wrong to. The verdict leaves that pattern on
\result{FlipToBetweenBn} billion dollars of additional debt-financed capital spending, which is
\result{FlipToBetweenShare} percent of the commitments already identified in tier 2, and reaches
the debt-led pattern on \result{FlipToDebtLedBn} billion, \result{FlipToDebtLedShare} percent of
them. The drawn portion of those commitments alone already exceeds the first threshold, and tier 4
is unmeasured.

\begin{quote}
The defensible statement is that on the nine filers' own balance sheets the structure resembles
the equity-led case. The statement that the structure resembles the equity-led case is not
defensible, and we do not make it.
\end{quote}

\subsection{The bust through the same engine}

Converted to a wage-bill equivalent, a bust costs \result{BustWageLow} to \result{BustWageHigh}
percent of the wage bill, \result{BustRatioLow} to \result{BustRatioHigh} times smaller than the
ten percent displacement case. Household credit losses are \result{BustCreditLow} to
\result{BustCreditHigh} billion dollars, and output falls \result{BustGDPLow} to
\result{BustGDPHigh} percent, well inside the supervisory severely adverse scenario. Federal
receipts, by contrast, fall \result{BustReceiptsLow} to \result{BustReceiptsHigh} billion dollars
on the verified historical episodes: an order of magnitude larger than the terminal-year fiscal
loss at ten percent displacement, and arriving through capital gains and corporate receipts rather
than wage taxes. These are scenario figures.

Why the bust transmits so weakly to households is measured, and it is the same ownership fact that
drove Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}. Corporate equity is extraordinarily concentrated: the top one
percent of households hold \result{TopOnePctEquity} percent of it and the bottom half
\result{BottomHalfEquity} percent \citep{FRBDFA}. A fall in equity value lands on the households
with the lowest propensity to consume out of wealth, measured at \result{MPCWealth} cents in the
dollar per year \citep{ChodorowReichNenovSimsek2021}. The concentration that makes the capital tax
base hard to reach makes the wealth channel weak. Two caveats: that propensity is a sourced
parameter checked against two episodes rather than one we estimated, and the output path does not
feed bank-channel damage back into itself, so the output fall is understated by an amount we have
not sized.

\subsection{The payoff by holder, and what a holdings table cannot show}

Figure~\ref{fig:payoff} scores each holder by its share of the two sides and reports the net
position in three outcomes. Only the federal government and banks lose in every column; everyone
else holds enough of the AI side to offset what they lose on the wage side if AI succeeds.
Households in particular hold \result{HouseholdsAISide} percent of the AI side against six percent
of the wage side, gaining \result{HouseholdPayoffSuccess} under success where the federal
government loses \result{FedPayoffSuccess}.

The figure understates the federal position in the first column, and the reason matters more than
the figure. It scores holders by what they hold, so a government holding about
\result{AIFederalShareRounded} percent of the AI side scores as barely exposed when AI fails, at
\result{FedPayoffFails}. But federal receipts fall \result{BustReceiptsLow} to
\result{BustReceiptsHigh} billion dollars in exactly that case. The state's claim on the AI upside
is fiscal rather than proprietary: it is the capital tax of Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}, not a
portfolio. Being barely exposed to a bust in holdings terms and being unhedged on the upside are
the same statement about a government whose only claim on AI capital is a tax reaching
\result{TauKBarkai} percent of the surplus.

\subsection{Two directions, two tax bases}

The instruments that would protect against the two failure directions are nearly disjoint, but not
because a bust leaves the wage side untouched. It touches it, by the amount reported above, and
the point is that it reaches the wage side through a channel one to two orders of magnitude
smaller than displacement does while reaching the federal budget through a different tax base
entirely. Instruments that protect the wage tax base do not protect the capital tax base, and the
reverse. One capital tax rate is simultaneously the state's only claim on the AI upside and the
thing that would have to rise to close the fiscal condition after displacement.
