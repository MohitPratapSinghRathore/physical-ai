\section{Data and method}
\label{sec:data}

The paper is assembled from five modules: the claim stock and its decomposition, a household
credit engine, a fiscal module, a second-round module, and an institution-level panel. This
section states what each is built from, what it holds fixed, and how a reader should weigh the
figures that come out of it. It ends with the replication protocol and the status vocabulary,
because those decide what may be said about any number in the paper.

\subsection{Sources and vintages}

Claim levels and holder shares come from the Federal Reserve financial accounts (Z.1), read from
the published data package rather than assembled series by series. The instrument suffix in Z.1 is
not the same across sectors, so bank, insurer and central bank holdings of the same instrument sit
under different codes; matching on the exact suffix silently pushes whole holder classes into an
unallocated remainder. We match on the instrument family and take the closest suffix per sector,
which cuts the aggregate remainder from about twenty-one percent to under two. Every series used
is recorded with the publisher's own description in the replication package.

The remaining sources, with the vintage each is read at: household balances from the Survey of
Income and Program Participation, 2025 panel, and household composition, earnings and occupation
from the American Community Survey public use microdata, 2023; official credit aggregates from the
New York Federal Reserve Household Debt and Credit report, 2026Q2, and the Federal Reserve's
consumer credit release; the wage share of adjusted gross income from Internal Revenue Service
Statistics of Income Table 1.4, tax year 2023; the wage bill, federal receipts and national
accounts aggregates from the national income accounts, with wages and salaries of
\result{WageBillBn} billion dollars and federal current receipts of \result{FedReceiptsBn} billion
dollars as the two bases used throughout; reemployment after displacement from fourteen biennial
vintages of the Bureau of Labor Statistics \emph{Displaced Workers Summary}; bank loss rates and
scenario severities from the Federal Reserve's 2026 supervisory stress test; trust fund income
from the 2026 Trustees Reports; and the AI side from the ten-K filings of nine SEC registrants.

Two conventions on sources are worth stating because they caused errors that the checks caught.
Units are taken from the provider at the point of retrieval rather than assumed, after a series
published in millions was read as billions. And a discontinued series is replaced rather than
carried: the auto loan aggregate moved to the New York Federal Reserve workbook after the previous
source stopped publishing, which changed the under-reporting factor for that book and nothing
downstream, because the same aggregate enters the correction and the bank-held share and cancels.

\subsection{The household credit engine}

The engine maps a displacement level onto household credit losses without ever naming a set of
displaced households. Displacement is a probability applied to every household in the working
core, which is a household containing at least one employed member aged 25 to 64. The share of
earners displaced is set equal to the share of the wage bill displaced, and each household's
earners are drawn against that share, so a household with two earners can lose one or both.

A household that loses an earner does not default; it becomes more likely to default. The
probability uplift is taken from the literature on job loss and mortgage default: five percentage
points for one displaced earner and eight for two, which is less than twice the first because the
second spell adds less than the first. Losses are then exposure at default times loss given
default, and the loss given default ranges are taken from the supervisory stress test and from the
conventional severities for each book: 0.25 to 0.40 on first-lien mortgages, 0.80 to 1.00 on
cards, 0.45 to 0.65 on auto and 0.75 to 1.00 on student debt. Reporting a portfolio loss rate
against exposure at default instead would apply the probability of default twice, which in an
early version of this engine made the credit channel appear more than twenty times smaller than it
is.

Survey balances are benchmarked to official aggregates before any of this. Two ratios do different
work and must not be multiplied into one. The \emph{under-reporting factor} is the official
aggregate over the full survey universe and is a survey correction. The \emph{coverage share} is
the working-core balance over the full survey universe and is a fact about which households the
analysis covers. Table~\ref{tab:underreporting} gives both for each book. Their identity, the
factor divided by the coverage share reproducing the working-core scale-up, holds to three
decimals on every book, and all four factors are replicated.

One scope statement travels with every credit number in this paper. The engine is first round
only: it holds house prices, consumer spending, business revenue and the employment of everyone
not displaced fixed. Compared against a supervisory scenario, which moves all of those together,
the figures are a lower bound on losses rather than an estimate of them.

\subsection{The fiscal module}

The fiscal module asks what a fall in the wage bill does to federal receipts. Displacement is
converted to a dollar amount against the wage bill, and the loss per dollar has three parts: the
labor tax no longer collected on wages that are not re-earned, less the capital tax collected on
the income that displaced them, plus any outlay response. The wage that is re-earned is governed by
a reemployment rate fitted on the fourteen displaced-worker vintages against labor market slack,
and solved as a fixed point against a stock-flow identity rather than read off the line, because
displacement changes the slack that the line is a function of.

Two choices in that module matter enough to state here. The loss is reported for the terminal year
rather than as a cumulative total divided by a horizon, because a cumulative loss is a stock and an
annual loss is a flow, and only the second is comparable with a budget. And the loss is not linear
in displacement: the share of wages re-earned falls as displacement rises, so the fiscal loss rises
faster than the displacement that produces it. Trust fund effects are computed on a payroll-only
basis with each fund's own payroll share of its own income, since the two funds have different
shares and using one for the other overstates the smaller fund's ratio by about five percent.

\subsection{The second-round module, and why it is scenario}

The second round is the part of the paper with the weakest evidential basis, and it is labelled
scenario everywhere it appears. The chain is short and stated in full: the net fall in wage income
becomes a demand shortfall through the difference between the propensity to consume out of labor
income and out of capital income; the shortfall is expressed as a share of output and compared
with the fall in output in the Federal Reserve's severely adverse scenario; and the Federal
Reserve's own published loss totals are scaled by that ratio. Business credit, commercial real
estate and card losses enter only through that mapping. House prices enter separately, through an
income elasticity applied to the fall in the national wage bill.

Three things in that chain have no source. The shape of the loss curve beyond the supervisory
point is unknown, so we carry three mappings, one linear, one that refuses to extrapolate beyond
the supervisory severity, and one convex, and report the spread between them rather than choosing.
The propensity ranges are measured on transitory shocks while displacement is persistent, so the
spread between the labor and capital propensities is if anything understated. The results are
reported over the whole grid of 648 combinations, as a band. A point estimate inside that band
would be false precision, and we do not report one.

\subsection{Institution-level data}

The second round is applied across individual balance sheets rather than to a system aggregate.
The panel is every FDIC-insured bank and every federally insured credit union at 30 June 2026,
with the loan book by category and the capital measure each one is tested against: the tier 1
leverage ratio against its four percent threshold for banks, the net worth ratio against its six
percent threshold for credit unions. After exclusions this is \result{NInstitutions} institutions,
\result{NBanks} banks and \result{NCreditUnions} credit unions, holding \result{SystemAssetsBn}
billion dollars of assets.

\result{ExcludedInstitutions} institutions holding \result{ExcludedAssetsBn} billion dollars are
excluded, and the reason is worth stating because it was found by a check rather than by
inspection. They report neither tier 1 capital nor total equity, and they are overwhelmingly
insured US branches of foreign chartered institutions whose capital is held at the parent. A
capital test applied to them shows them breaching before any loss is applied, which is an artifact
of the missing field rather than a finding about the institution. The baseline check is what
surfaced them, and a capital test is only informative if almost nothing fails it before any loss:
after the exclusion, \result{BaselineBreaches} institutions breach at baseline, which is
\result{BaselinePctAssets} percent of system assets. All counts in this paper are post-exclusion
and the excluded institutions are named in the replication package.

Loss allocation across the panel is pro rata by category holdings, so the dispersion in the
results reflects differences in business mix and nothing else. True dispersion, which would also
reflect differences in underwriting and borrower composition within a category, is wider. The
institution-level distributions are therefore a lower bound on how uneven the outcome would be.

\subsection{The claims register and the status vocabulary}

Every claim the project has made is carried in a public register with its status, the artifact
that produced it, and a separate flag recording whether it has been independently rebuilt. Status
is not a matter of judgement at the point of writing: it is assigned by a clearing pass whose
source of truth is a single released file, and no claim reaches this manuscript without a status
in that file. Claims tagged as removed or withdrawn appear nowhere in the paper except
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, which lists them with the reason.

Separately, a standing audit states the bound a quantity must respect before the quantity is
computed, and checks it afterwards. A loss on a payroll tax cannot exceed that tax base; a share
cannot leave the unit interval; a debt-only denominator cannot exceed an all-claims denominator.
The audit runs \result{PlausChecks} checks with \result{PlausViolations} violations, all of which
are superseded rows retained deliberately so that the audit records what was corrected rather than
quietly passing.

\subsection{Three rebuild rounds, described exactly}

Round one established the protocol and produced no comparable values in several modules, because
the brief it worked from left definitions unstated. Round two scored \result{RepTwoScored}
quantities, \result{RepTwoSealed} of them against expected values sealed in advance, by an
instance that read none of the project code and worked from raw data and a published brief alone.
\result{RepTwoFivePct} came within five percent and \result{RepTwoTolerance} inside the tolerance
stated before the round began; \result{RepTwoMismatch} did not match, and the six root causes are
published with the round. Round three covered the two constructions the earlier rounds had not
reached, the debt-only ratio and the assembled capital tax rate, and matched all
\result{RepThreeSealed} sealed quantities, \result{RepThreeRounding} of them within rounding, with
\result{RepThreeMismatch} mismatches.

The protocol has a limitation that we state rather than let a reader discover. The blind was
procedural, not enforced. The rebuilder worked on the same machine, and the sealed file was
reachable at a path the brief itself names; it was not attached to the request, and the rebuilder
reported it unopened until the rebuild was final. The supporting evidence is internal and
circumstantial: most of the insufficiencies were written before the file was opened, several of
the mismatch causes were among them, and the rebuilder declined to use coefficients the brief had
leaked. On that basis the claim in this paper is that a quantity was \emph{independently rebuilt
under a reported blind protocol}, and never that it was independently verified.

That gives the three labels used throughout. \emph{Replicated} means rebuilt in round two or round
three inside the stated tolerance. \emph{Measured} means produced by the pipeline, clearing every
applicable check, but not independently rebuilt. \emph{Scenario} means arithmetic conditional on
an assumption that is stated where the number appears. Nothing else is reported.

\subsection{Disclosure of AI assistance}

The pipeline, the analysis code and the drafting of this manuscript were carried out with
substantial assistance from a generative AI coding assistant, and the replication rounds were
performed by separate instances of the same class of tool working from published briefs. The
authors specified the questions, the conventions and the checks, reviewed every output, and take
full responsibility for the content. No figure in this paper was produced by a language model
asserting a value: every reported number is generated from a measured artifact in the replication
package by the macro pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:accounts}, and the build fails if the
prose calls for a value that no artifact defines.
