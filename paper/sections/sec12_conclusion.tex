\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The United States financial system carries a large stock of claims whose servicing depends on
labor income. About \result{DebtOnlyDirect} percent of all US debt, public and private, is
serviced directly from wages. Counting the federal government as exposed when it is holder,
guarantor or debtor, it is exposed on about \result{SovereignUnion} percent of those claims, and
on about \result{AIFederalShareRounded} percent of the claims on AI capital that we can observe.
That second figure is an upper bound on a side we can only measure from below.

Displacement of wage income is a fiscal event before it is a financial one. At the one level fully
inside the observed data, the federal government bears roughly four fifths of the first-round loss
while the banking system barely moves, and the households with the least room are one quintile up
from the bottom. Whether the budget absorbs that turns on which capital tax rate reaches AI
profits. The condition needs \result{RequiredEasy} to \result{RequiredHard} percent, and the income
that moves from wages to AI capital bears \result{TauKBarkai} percent once every layer of tax is
counted once. It falls short at the center on both defensible bases for the share of equity the
income tax reaches.

The state loses in both directions, through different tax bases: wage taxes if AI succeeds,
capital gains and corporate taxes if it fails. So one capital tax rate is being asked to do two
opposite jobs. That, rather than any single loss estimate, is what the measurement is for. The
paper is written for the people who have to answer such questions with numbers, and everything in
it is generated from artifacts in a replication package, each carrying its status, so that a
reader who rejects a convention can change it and see what moves.
