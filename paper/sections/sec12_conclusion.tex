\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Wage-dependent public revenues and credit guarantees concentrate automation exposure on the
federal balance sheet. About \result{DebtOnlyDirect} percent of all US debt, public and private,
is serviced directly from wages. Counting the federal government as exposed when it is holder,
guarantor or debtor, it is exposed on about \result{SovereignUnion} percent of those claims. The
accounts, and the holder map that comes with them, are what the paper contributes.

Displacement of wage income is a fiscal event before it is a financial one. In the calibrated
scenario with the least extrapolation, the federal government bears roughly four fifths of the
first-round loss while the banking system barely moves. The households with the least room are one
quintile up from the bottom. Whether the budget absorbs that turns on the capital tax that reaches
the income replacing wages. On a consistently federal basis that rate is
\result{TauKFederalOnly} percent, against the \result{RequiredFedEasy} to
\result{RequiredFedHard} percent replacement requires. The condition closes only where taxable
capital income per displaced wage dollar reaches \result{GStarFedEasy} to
\result{GStarFedHard}, above the ceiling of one that output-preserving displacement implies.

The federal government is fiscally exposed in both states, through different tax bases: wage taxes
if AI succeeds, capital gains and corporate taxes if it fails. That, rather than any single loss
estimate, is what the measurement is for. Everything here is generated from artifacts in a
replication package, each carrying its status, so a reader who rejects a convention can change it
and see what moves.
