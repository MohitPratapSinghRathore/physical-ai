\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Wage-dependent public revenues and credit guarantees concentrate automation exposure on the
federal balance sheet. About \result{DebtOnlyDirect} percent of all US debt, public and private,
is serviced directly from wages. Counting the federal government as exposed when it is holder,
guarantor or debtor, it is exposed on about \result{SovereignUnion} percent of those claims. On
the separately measured claims on AI capital its share is about
\result{AIFederalShareRounded} percent, an upper bound on a side we can measure only from below.
The two are answers to different questions and we do not net them.

Displacement of wage income is a fiscal event before it is a financial one. In the calibrated scenario with the least
extrapolation, the federal government bears roughly four fifths of the first-round loss
while the banking system barely moves, and the households with the least room are one quintile up
from the bottom. Whether the budget absorbs that turns on which capital tax rate reaches AI
profits. The condition needs \result{RequiredEasy} to \result{RequiredHard} percent, and the income
that moves from wages to AI capital bears \result{TauKBarkai} percent once every layer of tax is
counted once. It falls short at the center on both defensible bases for the share of equity the
income tax reaches.

The federal government is fiscally exposed in both states, through different tax bases: wage taxes
if AI succeeds, capital gains and corporate taxes if it fails. That, rather than any single loss
estimate, is what the measurement is for. The paper is written for the people who have to answer
such questions with numbers, and everything in it is generated from artifacts in a replication
package, each carrying its status, so that a reader who rejects a convention can change it and see
what moves.
