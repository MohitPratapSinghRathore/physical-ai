\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We introduce labor backing accounts, a decomposition of all United States financial claims by
the income that services each claim and by who ultimately holds it. About \result{DebtOnlyDirect}
percent of all US debt, public and private, is serviced directly from wages, and
\result{DebtOnlyIndirect} percent once indirect servicing is counted. Government debt enters
this measure through the labor-linked share of the receipts that service it, not because a
household owes it. The share has been stable since 1952. Mapping the holders shows an
asymmetry: the federal government is exposed, as holder, guarantor or debtor, on about
\result{SovereignUnion} percent of directly wage-backed claims, and on roughly one percent of
the claims on artificial intelligence capital. It therefore holds the wage side of a two-sided
bet on AI and almost none of the other side. We then ask whether the tax system can close the
gap, and build the effective marginal rate on AI surplus from components with every layer of
tax counted once. It is \result{TauKBarkai} percent under one reading of the rent share and
\result{TauKKN} percent under a second, incompatible reading, against the
\result{RequiredLow} to \result{RequiredHigh} percent needed to replace the wage taxes lost.
The binding reason is ownership rather than offshore profit shifting: about
\result{ThetaUntaxable} percent of US corporate equity is held by pension funds, retirement
accounts, foreign investors and nonprofits, and about \result{GainsUntaxedAtDeath} percent of
capital gains are never taxed at all. We label what is measured and
what is scenario, and release a replication package.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} fiscal exposure; automation; labor share; capital taxation;

\section{Introduction}

A government that guarantees mortgages, lends for education, and funds itself from payroll and
income taxes has taken a position on wages without ever describing it as one. If automation
moves income from labor to capital, that position loses value on both sides of the public
balance sheet at once: the revenue that services the debt falls, and the credit that the
government has guaranteed deteriorates for the same reason. Whether the government holds an
offsetting claim on the capital that replaced the labor is an empirical question, and it is the
question this paper answers.

The answer is that it does not. We measure the United States as holding the wage side of a
two-sided bet on artificial intelligence and almost none of the other side. If AI succeeds and
displaces labor, wage taxes fall and wage-backed credit deteriorates. If AI fails, capital gains
and corporate receipts fall instead. The federal government loses in both directions, through
different tax bases, and in neither direction does it hold a claim large enough to offset the
loss. That is a structural observation about who holds what, not a forecast about which
direction events will take.

Three numbers carry the argument. First, about \result{DebtOnlyDirect} percent of all US debt,
public and private, is serviced directly from wages, and \result{DebtOnlyIndirect} percent once
indirect servicing is counted. Second, the federal government is exposed on about
\result{SovereignUnion} percent of the directly wage-backed claims, as holder, guarantor or
debtor, against roughly one percent of the claims on AI capital. Third, income that moves from
wages to AI capital bears an effective marginal tax rate of about \result{TauKBarkai} percent,
while replacing the wage taxes lost would require \result{RequiredLow} to \result{RequiredHigh}
percent.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes four.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.25em]
\item \textbf{A measurement framework.} Labor backing accounts decompose all US financial claims
two ways at once, by the income that services them and by who ultimately holds them. The
framework yields a ratio, a sovereign exposure share, a holder map for both sides of the bet, a
series running from 1952, and a breakdown by wage quintile. The construction, the conventions,
and the sensitivity of each to the judgement calls behind it are stated in full.

\item \textbf{A holder map that shows the asymmetry.} The wage side and the AI side are measured
on the same basis so that the exposures can be compared rather than asserted. This is where the
one-sidedness becomes visible.

\item \textbf{An explanation of why the tax system cannot close the gap.} We rebuild the
effective marginal rate on AI surplus from components, taking the treatment of full expensing
directly from \citet{AcemogluManeraRestrepo2020} rather than assuming it, and we show that the
binding constraint is the ownership of corporate claims rather than offshore profit shifting.
About \result{ThetaUntaxable} percent of US corporate equity sits with holders the individual
income tax does not reach, and about \result{GainsUntaxedAtDeath} percent of capital gains are
never taxed because basis is stepped up at death.

\item \textbf{A measured incidence of displacement.} At the only displacement level inside the
observed data, we measure where the first-round loss lands across the federal government,
households, housing agencies and \result{NInstitutions} bank and credit union balance sheets.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.} We do not forecast the pace of automation. We do not claim
that AI will displace any particular quantity of labor, and the paper reports a dose-response
surface rather than a point on it. Only one displacement level, ten percent of the wage bill,
sits inside the range of the data used to calibrate reemployment; every figure above that level
is labeled as scenario arithmetic and is reported as a band. We do not claim that the
measurement framework is new to the literature in the strong sense; our search located no prior
construction of the same object, but a systematic search has not been completed, so the novelty
claim is stated as ``none located'' rather than ``none exists''. We do not claim that the
capital tax result settles the question of which tax base is the right one; we argue for a
marginal, flow-based rate and state the case against it plainly in Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}.

\paragraph{Research questions and objectives.} RQ1 asks what share of US financial claims is
serviced by labor income, and who holds those claims; RO1 constructs the labor backing accounts
and the holder map, in Section~\ref{sec:accounts}. RQ2 asks whether the capital tax system can
replace the revenue lost when income shifts from wages to AI capital; RO2 builds the effective
marginal rate from components and compares it with the required rate, in
Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}. RQ3 asks where the loss from displacement lands, by holder; RO3
measures first-round incidence at the level inside the data, in Section~\ref{sec:displacement}.
RQ4 asks what the second round does to the institutions that hold the credit; RO4 applies the
losses across individual balance sheets, in Section~\ref{sec:banks}. RQ5 asks what happens in the
opposite direction, if AI disappoints; RO5 prices that case in Section~\ref{sec:bust}.
