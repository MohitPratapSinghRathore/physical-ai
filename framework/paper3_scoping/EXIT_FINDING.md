## The finding

**The United States has escaped a concentrated position in wage-backed claims before. The
route it used is now closed.**

### 1. The 1947–1965 exit, measured

The sovereign share of the labour-backed claim stock fell from **0.696 to 0.308** in eighteen
years. It did not fall because the state shrank:

| 1947 → 1965 | Growth |
|---|---|
| Labour-backed claim stock | **×3.30** |
| **Treasury debt** | **×1.40** |
| **Household credit** | **×7.61** |

Treasury grew 1.40× in nominal terms — it never shrank. **The state's share fell because the
household sector expanded around a near-static Treasury.** Treasury's share of the
labour-backed stock went from 0.692 to 0.293; households went from 0.270 to 0.622.

This is not the financial-repression story. Inflation eroding debt-to-GDP is a statement
about the numerator. This is a statement about the denominator: **private wage-backed debt
was substituted for public.**

### 2. Why it cannot happen again

In 1947–65 the state guaranteed almost none of the household credit that diluted it:

| Year | State share of household credit |
|---|---|
| 1947 | **0.0150** |
| 1965 | **0.0204** |
| 1980 | 0.1379 |
| 2000 | 0.3806 |
| 2007 | 0.3548 |
| **2025** | **0.5576** |

**The state now holds or guarantees 56 percent of household credit. In 1965 it was 2
percent.** So household credit expansion no longer dilutes the state's position — it carries
the state with it.

The counterfactual, holding Treasury fixed and applying each era's guarantee rate to new
household credit:

| Household credit grows | Sovereign share if the state guarantees 0% of it (1947–65) | …if it guarantees 56% (today) |
|---|---|---|
| ×1 (today) | 0.7936 | 0.7936 |
| ×2 | 0.5714 | 0.7275 |
| ×3 | 0.4464 | 0.6903 |
| ×5 | 0.3106 | 0.6499 |
| **×7.61** (the 1947–65 expansion) | **0.2223** | **0.6237** |

**Repeating the exact expansion that worked last time would leave the state at 0.624 instead
of 0.222.** The route is not merely harder. It is structurally closed by the state's own
guarantee book.

### 3. And the expansion itself is unavailable

The household-condition workstream already established that US households cannot absorb a
large further claim: 84.6 percent of bottom-quartile households lose debt-service capacity
under a modest labour-to-capital shift, against 23.6 percent of the top 1 percent, and 47
percent of corporate equity never reaches a household at all. A ×5 household credit expansion
is not a live possibility.

**So both conditions for the historical exit fail: the expansion cannot happen, and if it did
it would no longer dilute.**

## Why this survives the mistake that killed the rotation

`K4_RESULT.md` withdrew the session 3 "rotation" because a share change was read as a
statement about behaviour when the denominator was moving underneath it. **This finding is
constructed to avoid that:**

- The 1947–65 numbers are reported as **growth multiples**, not share changes: Treasury ×1.40,
  household credit ×7.61, stock ×3.30. The share fall is then explained *by* those, not
  asserted from them.
- The guarantee rate (2 percent → 56 percent) is **state-held household credit over household
  credit**. Numerator and denominator are the same population, so it is a genuine ratio and
  not a composition artefact.
- The counterfactual is explicitly arithmetic and labelled as such. It says what an expansion
  *would* do, not what anything *did*.

## What has to happen before this is a paper

**A novelty check, and it is the binding constraint.** Every candidate in this project has
been killed or narrowed by one, and the base rate is now: eight hypotheses tested, one narrow
survivor, one artefact withdrawn.

The specific question: has anyone characterised the postwar decline in the government's share
of credit as a **substitution of private household debt for public**, and has anyone observed
that the growth of federal guarantees closed that channel?

Adjacent literatures that will bear on it, and which I expect to narrow the claim:

- Reinhart and Sbrancia on financial repression and postwar debt liquidation — the numerator
  story, and very well established.
- The postwar consumer and mortgage credit literature — the household expansion is thoroughly
  documented on its own.
- Debt sustainability and fiscal space — the forward-looking question in other terms.

**The honest prior** is that the two halves are each documented and the contribution is again
the common denominator plus the joining of them, which is exactly the position sessions 1 and
3 reached. What is different here is that the joining produces a **forward-looking claim that
neither half yields alone**, and that claim is falsifiable and policy-relevant.

**Realistic target if it survives: the same lower-Q1 band already written down.** I am not
raising it, and nothing in this session changes my standing advice that the measurement paper
is the asset.
