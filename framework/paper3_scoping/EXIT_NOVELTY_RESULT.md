# Novelty result: the exit finding is dead

**2026-09-26. Criterion in `EXIT_NOVELTY_CRITERION.md`, written before the search.**

## Verdict

**KILL-A fires. Claim 1 is published annually in the President's Budget.**

**KILL-B does not fire.** Claim 2 survives the search. But claim 2 cannot carry a paper on
its own, for reasons in §3, and the finding is therefore **withdrawn as a paper candidate**.

---

## 1. KILL-A: the substitution story is official reference material

**Office of Management and Budget, *Analytical Perspectives*, Budget of the United States,
Table 15-1.** Verified by direct fetch of the FY2005 edition.

It publishes **federal debt held by the public as a percentage of total credit market debt**:

| Year | Federal share of credit market debt |
|---|---|
| 1950 | **53.3%** |
| 1975 | **18.4%** |
| 2001 | 17.5% |

And it explains the decline in these words:

> "With households borrowing large amounts to buy homes and consumer durables, and with
> businesses borrowing large amounts to buy plant and equipment, Federal debt also decreased
> almost every year as a percentage of the total credit market debt outstanding."

**That is claim 1, on a common denominator, stated in the federal budget documents.** Not a
research finding to be discovered — reference material, republished every year.

My version measured the same substitution on a labour-backed denominator instead of total
credit market debt, and over 1947–65 rather than 1950–75. The denominator and the window
differ. The **finding** does not.

*(The CRS report RL34712, which a search summary suggested carried this, does not: fetched and
checked, it reports federal debt only against GDP. The figures belong to OMB. Recorded so the
attribution is right.)*

**Related and also established:** Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen show safe and liquid
government debt crowds out financial-sector short-term debt, verified on US data 1875–2014.
Public and private claim supply being substitutes is a mature theoretical result.

## 2. KILL-B: does not fire

No source was found arguing that the growth of federal credit guarantees **forecloses** the
deleveraging channel, or that the state's guarantee book makes private credit expansion
non-dilutive to its position. The contingent-liability literature exists — CBO on federal
credit and insurance commitments, IMF on government guarantees and fiscal risk — but it sizes
exposures rather than asking what they do to the state's ability to dilute out of a position.

Recorded as an absence, which is weak evidence, not as established novelty.

## 3. Why claim 2 cannot carry a paper alone

The finding's force came entirely from the pairing: *it happened before, and it cannot now.*
With the first half published in the budget documents, what remains is:

> The state holds or guarantees 0.5576 of household credit against 0.0204 in 1965, so
> household credit expansion no longer dilutes its position.

Three problems.

**It is close to a restatement of the guarantee rate.** Once you know the state guarantees 56
percent of household credit, "expanding household credit expands the state's exposure" is
almost arithmetic. The counterfactual table dresses it up; it does not add to it.

**The guarantee rate itself was already narrowed twice**, in session 1: FHFA publishes a GSE
share series on a mortgage denominator, and CBO publishes federal credit and insurance
commitments against household financial assets. The 0.5576 is a recombination of material
that is already published.

**And OMB's own series undercuts the framing.** Federal debt is **17.5 percent** of credit
market debt in 2001 against 18.4 percent in 1975 — essentially flat — while my labour-backed
series rises over the same period. A referee's first question is why the two disagree, and the
answer is the denominator plus the guarantee leg. That is the same defence used in sessions 1,
3 and here, and it has now been asked to carry three different papers.

## 4. Where this leaves the project

**Nine hypotheses tested across this workstream. One narrow survivor, one artefact withdrawn,
and this one killed.**

| | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Prediction (validation pilot) | null, pre-registered, published as a limitation |
| Euro-area sovereign share | fail, 10.9pp against a 15pp threshold |
| Household ownership constraint | fail, already modelled |
| Descriptive co-movement | fail, definitional or unstable |
| Regulatory-treatment gap | **narrow survivor**, twice narrowed, Acharya 2011 closest |
| Theory: factor mismatch / leverage / coverage | fail, three of four framings |
| Paper 2, the two-phase absorption | Section 4.5, not a paper |
| The rotation | **withdrawn**, denominator artefact |
| **The exit** | **killed**, OMB Table 15-1 |

**The pattern is stable and worth stating plainly.** Every attempt to make the labour backing
accounts important *by attaching them to something else* has failed, been done before, or
turned out to be an artefact. The one consistent result across nine attempts is that the
accounts are a good measurement and a poor lever.

**My advice is unchanged and I do not think further search has positive expected value.** The
measurement paper — the sovereign concentration, its doubling, the 47 percent of equity that
never reaches a household, the liquidity-versus-ownership result — is the asset. It is worth
publishing as what it is.
