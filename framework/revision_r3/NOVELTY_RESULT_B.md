# NOVELTY_RESULT_B: verdict against the criteria fixed in NOVELTY_PROTOCOL_B

**2026-10-08. Criteria committed at `f445449` before any search was run.**

## Verdict

**PROCEED, REFRAMED.** KILL-1 fires, KILL-2 does not, KILL-3 does not. The analytic claim must
be demoted to standard method; the paper survives on the computation and the measurement. The
results fall in a cell the protocol did not quite anticipate, and that is recorded below rather
than resolved by bending a criterion.

## Each criterion, answered

**KILL-1, is the mapping textbook? FIRES.** The distinction between a marginal effective tax
rate and an average or annual effective rate is established public finance, and so is the part
that matters here. Sorensen's survey on estimating effective corporate tax rates states that
"it is easy to confuse the AETR and METR since they appear to be minor arithmetic differences"
and gives the relationship directly: the AETR is a weighted average of the METR and the
statutory corporate rate, with the weight on the statutory rate rising with the ratio of average
to marginal return. That is the same structure as our rent-share weighting, and the corollary
that expensing nulls the marginal rate on normal returns while the average rate on existing
capital stays positive is standard.

**Consequence: the paper may not present the distinction as a finding.** It is a standard tool,
correctly applied to a question where it has not been applied. The current title and abstract
lead with the distinction and must be rewritten.

**KILL-2, is the shortfall claim our own invention? DOES NOT FIRE.** The claim is prominent in
print and is made by others, including quantitatively:

- Oxford Economics (2025) estimates automation could reduce government tax revenues by eight to
  eleven percent in developed economies by 2035 absent policy change.
- *Tax Journal* states that "unless corporate profits were to increase very substantially
  indeed, it would be necessary for the government to increase the corporation tax rate by a
  significant amount to recoup the personal tax revenues lost through automation". That is our
  condition, asserted in words.
- The same literature computes the left-hand side the way we do, noting that "a rough estimate
  of revenue loss can be arrived at by applying an effective tax rate to the gross salary loss
  due to automation", and McKinsey's displaced-wage figure is widely used for it.
- ITIF published a 2026 rebuttal titled "AI Is Not Going to Reduce Labor's Share of Income or
  Destroy the Tax Base", which is evidence the claim is mainstream enough to need rebutting.

So Paper B is not a corrigendum on an error this project invented. The claim it tests is live.

**KILL-3, does the decomposition exist? DOES NOT FIRE.** Nothing was located that decomposes
the non-taxable share of US corporate equity by eventual tax treatment and reports how much is
beyond all federal tax. The underlying tax facts are elementary and must be presented as such:
that distributions of pre-tax contributions and earnings from traditional arrangements are taxed
as ordinary income is black-letter law, not a discovery. What appears to be new is putting the
holder shares and the treatments together and reporting the residue, 8.8 percent, against the
headline figure of about 73 percent.

## The cell the protocol did not anticipate

PROCEED AS A PAPER was specified for the case where the shortfall claim is made in print by
others **on a marginal-wedge basis**. The claim is made in print by others, but not on that
basis: the sources assert it, or compute the revenue loss and leave the replacement side
qualitative. Nobody located assembles the right-hand side from effective rates at all.

That is a better position for the paper than the criterion contemplated, and we will not pretend
the criterion was met as written. The honest statement is that the contribution is **the
computation the debate has been doing without**, not the correction of a published computation.
The textbook status of the METR and AETR distinction then helps rather than hurts: the method is
standard, which is why the answer is hard to wave away.

## What the paper may and may not claim

**May claim.**
1. The first assembly, as far as we can find, of both sides of the wage-to-capital revenue
   replacement condition from sourced components, on a consistent jurisdictional basis.
2. That on the revenue-relevant rate the asserted shortfall does not hold: it closes at every
   corner, and on the marginal rate the remaining gap is under one point with a straddling
   range, so no shortfall is established on either object.
3. The shareholder-layer decomposition and the 8.8 percent residue.
4. That the rent-share disagreement, which drives the entire spread of the marginal rate,
   drops out of the annual object, so the Barkai and Karabarbounis-Neiman readings stop
   mattering once the right object is used.

**May not claim.**
1. That the marginal-versus-annual distinction is new. It is standard and must be cited as such.
2. That any specific published calculation is being corrected. None careful enough was located.
3. That the paper settles which object belongs in the condition, which needs a displacement
   horizon we do not measure.

- Add the literature that makes the claim, so there is a named target, and the standard

## Addendum, same session: the conclusion is not new either, and that must be said

A further search located the paper that matters most, and it was not in the first pass.

**Hotte, Theodorakopoulos and Koutroumpis (2024), "Automation and taxation", Oxford Economic
Papers 76(4), 945, doi 10.1093/oep/gpae006.** The abstract asks "whether automation-induced
changes in labour and capital income undermine public revenues", decomposes taxes by source for
nineteen EU countries over 1995 to 2016, and reports that "there is no empirical evidence that
tax revenues are negatively affected by automation in the long run", with the answer depending
on the technology and the stage of diffusion.

Two consequences, and the second is the awkward one.

1. **The question is live and academic**, not merely a policy talking point. KILL-2 is confirmed
   by a better source than the ones first found: a reputable journal publishes the question in
   exactly the terms we pose it.
2. **Our conclusion is not new.** The best existing empirical work already finds no revenue
   erosion. We cannot present "no established shortfall" as a discovery. What we have is a
   different route to an answer someone else has reached: forward-looking and structural, on US
   data, assembling a replacement condition from effective rates, against their backward-looking
   reduced-form panel evidence from the EU.

Agreement between two methods that share no data and no identifying assumptions is worth
reporting, and it is the honest frame. It is also a weaker claim than the manuscript currently
makes.

## Revised statement of what the paper may claim

**May claim.**
1. The first assembly, as far as we can find, of both sides of the wage-to-capital revenue
   replacement condition from sourced components, forward-looking and on a consistent
   jurisdictional basis for the United States.
2. That the condition does not bind on the revenue-relevant rate, **corroborating Hotte et al.
   by an independent route**, and that on the marginal rate the gap is under one point with a
   straddling range.
3. The shareholder-layer decomposition and the 8.8 percent residue, which is the one piece of
   measurement we have not found elsewhere.
4. That the rent-share disagreement drops out of the annual object, so the Barkai and
   Karabarbounis-Neiman readings stop mattering once the right object is used.

**May not claim.**
1. That the marginal-versus-annual distinction is new. Cite Devereux and Griffith (2003),
   International Tax and Public Finance 10(2), 107 to 126, verified, for the effective average
   rate, and the standard effective-rate literature for the rest.
2. That no revenue shortfall is a new finding. Hotte et al. have it. We corroborate it.
3. That any specific published calculation is being corrected.
4. That the paper settles which object belongs in the condition.

**Standing.** The paper is a corroborating structural computation plus one new measurement. That
is a sound contribution and a modest one. It is a field-journal paper, not a top-five one, and
the earlier framing claimed considerably more than this.
