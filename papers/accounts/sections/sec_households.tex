\section{Households, by position in the wage distribution}
\label{sec:households}

The accounts carry one more cut. Because the household part of the wage-backed stock can be split
by wage quintile, and because the same survey gives the liquid assets of the same households, the
two can be set side by side. They answer different questions and they disagree, which is the
result of this section.

\subsection{Claims are spread more evenly than the income that services them}

The gradient is the finding. The statistic is an index, a quintile's share of household
wage-attributed claims over its share of the wage bill, and it falls as pay rises: from
\result{QTwoPerDollar} in the second quintile to \result{QTopPerDollar} at the top. Those are relative
figures, not dollars. Multiplying by the aggregate multiplier of \result{QAggMultiplier}, total
household wage-attributed claims over the total wage bill, gives \result{QTwoLevel} and
\result{QTopLevel} claims per wage dollar. We report the index in the text because the one contested
cell is disputed on that basis, and Table~\ref{tab:quintile} carries both. The top
quintile earns \result{QTopWageShare} percent of the wage bill and carries
\result{QTopClaimShare} percent of the household claims. Claims are spread far more evenly than
the income attributed to servicing them. Table~\ref{tab:quintile} gives the detail, and
Appendix~\ref{app:detail} the construction, including the one magnitude whose dispute we flag.

So the ratio of claims to wages rises as pay falls. That is an arithmetic property of the claim
structure, not a claim about how anyone behaves, and it is a statement about stocks rather than
about incidence. A proportional fall in pay would be regressive in welfare, in default or in
debt-service terms only given payment schedules, maturity, transfers, other assets and
expenditure, none of which these accounts carry. A household in the second quintile supplies about nine percent of the wage bill and
carries about thirteen percent of the household claims against it; a household in the top
quintile supplies half the wage bill and carries a third of the claims. Nothing has to happen for
that asymmetry to exist. It is there now.

\subsection{Where the buffers are thinnest, and it is not the bottom}

Liquid runway by wage quintile gives the second household result, and buffers are not monotonic
in pay: the thinnest are in the \emph{second} quintile, a median of \result{QTwoRunway} months
against \result{QOneRunway} in the bottom quintile, with \result{QTwoUnderOne} percent under one
month against \result{QOneUnderOne} percent. From there upward the gradient is monotonic,
reaching \result{QFiveRunway} months at the top.

The denominator decides what this statistic means, so it should be stated rather than assumed.
Runway here is liquid assets over monthly wage income, not over essential expenditure plus
required debt service. The two are different objects and they need not rank quintiles the same
way: a household with low income and low commitments can show a short runway on our definition
and a long one on the other. We therefore report the non-monotonicity as a measured property of
liquid assets relative to income, and we do not conclude from it that second-quintile households
are the most vulnerable, which would require expenditure, benefits, dependants and other
resources that the accounts do not carry. What the number supports is narrower: relief designed
on the assumption that liquidity rises monotonically with pay is designed against a pattern the
data do not show.

\subsection{What the two cuts say together, and what they do not}

The claims gradient and the buffer gradient are measured on the same households from the same
survey, and they do not line up. The claims index declines from the second quintile through the
fifth. Whether it declines from the bottom is unresolved, because the rebuild puts the bottom
quintile at 1.21 to 1.64, which straddles the second quintile. Liquid runway is lowest one
quintile above the bottom. \textbf{We therefore cannot establish that the group carrying the most
claims per wage dollar differs from the group with the least liquid room}, because identifying
the first requires the bottom quintile's rank. What the two gradients do show is that they are
not the same shape over the quintiles where both are settled.

One point of arithmetic belongs here because it is visible in Table~\ref{tab:quintile}. The
share columns are on common totals, so the bottom quintile implies an index of
\result{QOnePerDollar}, well above the second quintile's \result{QTwoPerDollar}, or \result{QOneLevel} claims
per wage dollar on the level basis. That cell is the one an independent rebuild disputed by a factor of two
and a half to three and a half, landing at 1.21 to 1.64. We have since specified the
construction and tested the obvious suspect: taking the wage-bill denominator from the same
survey file as the balances, rather than from a second survey with its own quintile cut-points,
moves the cell by \result{QOneAgreement} percent. The cross-survey mismatch was real and is now
repaired, and it is not what drives the disagreement, which we cannot explain. We report the
magnitude with that flag rather than suppressing it, and we rest nothing on it. Because 1.21 to
1.64 straddles the second quintile, the bottom quintile's rank is not established. What survives
is the decline from the second quintile downward, which both constructions and both denominators
agree on.

We therefore make no claim about whether the two cuts identify the same group or different
ones. Settling that needs the bottom quintile's rank on the claims side, which is exactly what
is unresolved. What the two gradients show is that they are not the same shape over the
quintiles where both are settled, and that is as far as the measurement goes. Why the second quintile
holds the thinnest buffers is not something the accounts can answer; a plausible reading, that
these are households with enough income to qualify for credit and not enough to build a buffer
against it, is a hypothesis this paper does not test.
