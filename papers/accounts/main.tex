\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Every financial claim is serviced out of some income stream, yet the national financial accounts
record who owes a claim and who holds it, never which income pays it. This paper adds that
dimension. Labor backing accounts decompose the stock of United States financial claims twice over, by the income that
services each claim and by who holds, guarantees or owes it, across thirteen claim classes and a
series running from 1947 to 2025. About \result{DebtOnlyDirect} percent of US debt, public and
private, is serviced directly from wages, and \result{DebtOnlyIndirect} percent once indirect
servicing is counted. Counting the federal government as exposed when it is holder, guarantor or
debtor, it stands behind about \result{SovereignUnion} percent of directly wage-backed claims,
against about \result{AIFederalShareRounded} percent of the separately measured claims on AI
capital. That federal share is not a recent development but a return: it traces a U from
\result{SovUnion1947} percent in 1947, and a large part of the recent rise is growth in federal
debt itself rather
than new exposure to households. Claims are spread far more evenly than the income that services
them, falling from \result{QTwoPerDollar} dollars of wage-backed claims per wage dollar in the
second quintile to \result{QTopPerDollar} at the top. We state every convention the accounts rest
on, move each one at a time, and report a pre-registered test in which the measure fails to
predict bank credit losses. The accounts are accounting, not a risk measure, and we present them
as such.
\end{abstract}
