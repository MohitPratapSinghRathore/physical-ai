\section{The fiscal condition}
\label{sec:fiscal}

This module supports the central claim on its revenue side. If the public sector holds the wage
side of the exposure, the question is whether the tax that reaches the capital replacing that
labor could replace the revenue lost. We build both sides from components and report the answer
under every reading we can defend.

\subsection{What replacement requires}

Not every dollar of displaced wages leaves the wage tax base, because some of the displaced are
re-employed, at a wage on average lower than the one they lost.

\begin{definition}[Retained wage share and the fiscal condition]
\label{def:condition}
Let $R \in [0,1]$ be the share of a displaced wage bill that returns as wages, $\tau_\ell$ the
effective tax rate on wages and $\tau_k$ the effective marginal rate on the capital income that
replaces them. Replacing the lost revenue requires
\begin{equation}
\tau_k \;\ge\; \tau_\ell\,(1 - R) \;\equiv\; \tau_k^{\ast},
\label{eq:condition}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_k^{\ast}$ is the required rate.
\end{definition}

Measured on fourteen biennial vintages of displaced worker data and solved as a fixed point
against the stock-flow identity of Section~\ref{sec:data}, $R = \result{RetainedWageShare}$. Two
defensible readings of $\tau_\ell$ exist, a narrower one from the automation tax literature and a
broader bottom-up one built from federal taxes over wages and salaries, giving
$\tau_k^{\ast}$ between \result{RequiredEasy} and \result{RequiredHard} percent. We carry both and
call the lower one the easier reading. The retained wage share and both bounds were reproduced by
the independent rebuild.

\paragraph{What $R$ is, and the weighting the product form assumes.} $R$ is a share of the
\emph{wage bill}, not of workers:
\begin{equation}
R \;=\; \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} w_i^{\text{new}}}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} w_i^{\text{old}}},
\label{eq:Rdef}
\end{equation}
over the set $\mathcal{D}$ of displaced workers, with $w_i^{\text{new}} = 0$ for those not
reemployed. It is often written as a product of a reemployment rate $\rho$ and a wage-retention
factor $\omega$, and that form is not an identity. Writing $r_i = w_i^{\text{new}}/w_i^{\text{old}}$
for the reemployed set $\mathcal{R}$, Equation~\ref{eq:Rdef} factors as
\begin{equation}
R \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{|\mathcal{R}|}{|\mathcal{D}|}}_{\rho}
\;\times\;
\underbrace{\frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}} w_i^{\text{old}} r_i}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}} w_i^{\text{old}}}}_{\omega}
\;\times\;
\underbrace{\frac{\overline{w^{\text{old}}}\big|_{\mathcal{R}}}{\overline{w^{\text{old}}}\big|_{\mathcal{D}}}}_{\kappa},
\label{eq:Rfactor}
\end{equation}
so $R = \rho\,\omega$ requires two things: that $\omega$ be the \emph{wage-weighted} mean
retention among the reemployed rather than the unweighted one, and that $\kappa = 1$, meaning the
reemployed are a wage-representative subset of the displaced. The second fails if reemployment is
correlated with prior pay. Where better-paid workers are reemployed at higher rates, $\kappa > 1$
and the product $\rho\,\omega$ understates the retained wage bill, which makes the condition
easier to satisfy rather than harder. \textbf{Our $R$ is the product, not the wage-bill ratio.} An earlier version of this
section claimed we computed Equation~\ref{eq:Rdef} directly and therefore needed no
independence assumption. That was wrong. The reported $R = \result{RetainedWageShare}$ is
$\rho\omega$ with $\rho = \result{RhoReemploy}$, the observed reemployment rate across the
biennial displaced-worker vintages, and $\omega = \result{OmegaRetention}$, the wage-retention
factor among the reemployed. So the figure does carry the $\kappa = 1$ assumption, and the
paper should not have said otherwise. We keep it because the correction runs against this
paper rather than for it: if better-paid workers are reemployed at higher rates then
$\kappa > 1$, the product understates the retained wage bill, the required rate falls and the
condition becomes easier to satisfy. Computing Equation~\ref{eq:Rdef} on linked old and new
wage bills is the right fix and we have not done it.

\subsection{Building the rate on the income that shifts}

\begin{definition}[The assembled effective rate]
\label{def:tauk}
Let $\sigma$ be the share of the return that is economic rent, $s$ the share of rents booked in
lower-taxed jurisdictions, $\tau_c$ the combined entity-level rate, $\theta$ the share of
corporate equity held in taxable accounts, $\delta$ the present-value factor for deferral between
accrual and realization, $\tau_s$ the statutory shareholder rate, $b$ the debt share of financing
and $\tau_b$ the effective rate on interest income. With the shareholder layer
$\tau_{sh} = \theta\,\delta\,\tau_s$ and the entity layer $e$ formed from $\tau_c$ and $s$,
\begin{equation}
\tau_k \;=\; \sigma\,\bigl[e + (1-e)\,\tau_{sh}\bigr]
\;+\;(1-\sigma)\,\bigl[(1-b)\,\tau_{sh} + b\,(\tau_b - \tau_c)\bigr].
\label{eq:tauk}
\end{equation}
The first bracket is the rate on rents; the second is the rate on the normal return.
\end{definition}

Two features of Equation~\eqref{eq:tauk} do most of the work and neither is our assumption.
Under full expensing the normal return is relieved at the entity level, so at the margin it bears
only the owner's tax \citep{AcemogluManeraRestrepo2020}, which is why the debt-financed term is
negative throughout the sourced range and why the budget office's measured rate on debt-financed
corporate investment, minus \result{CBODebtFinancedETR} percent, sits inside the interval our
components imply \citep{CBO2014}. The rent share is the second: \citet{Barkai2020} puts it at
\result{RentBarkai} and \citet{KarabarbounisNeiman2014} at zero, two readings of what has happened
to the corporate sector rather than two estimates of one quantity, so we report both and never
average them.

\paragraph{The base, and the case against our own choice.} We take the rate on the marginal flow
of US AI surplus, whoever holds the claim, because the question concerns the next dollar that
moves. Against that, an economy-wide average rate on capital income, around twenty percent for
advanced economies, is measured on a stock mostly neither fully expensed nor easily shifted; if AI
capital comes to look like the rest of the capital stock, that is the relevant rate. Which base
answers which question is a judgment, not a fact.

\subsection{Which government, and the answer on each}

The two sides of Equation~\eqref{eq:condition} have to be measured on the same government. The
labor tax readings are federal: both are built from federal taxes on wages, one from the
automation tax literature and one bottom-up from federal receipts over wages and salaries. The
assembled rate, however, adds an effective state corporate tax to the federal entity rate, so a
comparison between them as they stand mixes jurisdictions. We therefore report two consistent
versions and make the federal one the headline, because the claim this paper makes is about the
federal balance sheet.

On the \emph{federal-only} version the state corporate term is set to zero, giving an assembled
rate of \result{TauKFederalOnly} percent against a required \result{RequiredFedEasy} to
\result{RequiredFedHard} percent. That figure is this section's first pass, on the shareholder
layer as assembled here; Section~\ref{sec:annual} decomposes the layer and corrects it to
\result{CfBaseFed} percent, and every comparison in the paper's conclusions uses the corrected
value. On the \emph{all-government} version the state corporate term
stays and the labor side gains the state and local wage-linked tax, worth
\result{StateLocalLaborAddOn} percent of the wage bill, giving \result{TauKAllGovernment} percent
against \result{RequiredAllGovEasy} to \result{RequiredAllGovHard} percent. Making the
comparison consistent widens the gap on either version, because the state adds more to the labor
side than to the capital side. Under the second rent reading the assembled rate is
\result{TauKKN} percent on either jurisdiction.

This is a scenario result in the strict sense, since two components are swept rather than
sourced; Figure~\ref{fig:tauk} shows that sweep. The construction was reproduced in full by the
independent rebuild.

How far short depends on the base for $\theta$, and the two answers differ enough that we report
both. On our base, $\theta$ is measured over all US equity outstanding, foreign holders included,
because a foreign holder bears close to no US tax at the owner level and that dollar is still in
the surplus. On the alternative base, the same numerator over domestically held stock alone,
$\theta$ rises to \result{CRSTheta} percent and the central rate to \result{TauKCRSCentral}
percent.

The precise statement is the share of a stated grid in which the condition closes. That grid is
the set of swept components, each drawn uniformly over the range given for it in
Section~\ref{sec:data} and the replication package; the share is a property of that grid and of
the uniform weighting, and it is not a probability that the condition closes. On our base it is
\result{PassShareOursEasy} percent against the easier labor tax reading and
\result{PassShareOursHard} percent against the harder; on the domestic-holder base it is
\result{PassShareCRSEasy} and \result{PassShareCRSHard} percent. The corners of the space reach further than those shares
suggest: the analytic maximum is \result{AnalyticMaxOurs} percent on our base and
\result{AnalyticMaxCRS} percent on the other, so both exceed the easier required rate of
\result{RequiredEasy} percent, and the second exceeds the harder \result{RequiredHard} percent as
well. What holds on every base and under every reading of the rent share is that the central case
falls short \emph{on the marginal rate}, and that under the second rent reading it closes nowhere
in the swept space. Section~\ref{sec:annual} shows that none of this survives the move to the
annual rate, which is the one revenue replacement turns on.

\subsection{What would have to be true for the conclusion to reverse}

A shortfall is only interesting if it survives the assumptions that would close it, so we apply
each of those in turn and then together, all on the all-government version for comparability.
Treating the foreign-held share of equity as bearing a fifteen percent treaty withholding rate at
the owner level, rather than nothing, raises the assembled rate to \result{TauKWithholding}
percent; that treaty rate is a stated assumption and not a measurement. Taking the debt-financed
share from the filers' own books rather than the economy-wide stock ratio raises it to
\result{TauKLowDebtShare} percent, because the debt-financed term is negative. Applying both
together gives \result{TauKJointWeakening} percent, still below the required
\result{RequiredAllGovEasy} percent. The second rent reading moves in the opposite direction, to
\result{TauKKN} percent.

One further assumption works in the paper's favor and belongs here for that reason. The labor
tax rate is economy-wide, while displacement is not uniform across the wage distribution. Using
the payroll and income tax rates by wage quintile already in the release, the effective rate runs
from \result{LaborTaxQuintileLow} percent at the bottom to \result{LaborTaxQuintileHigh} percent
at the top, so displacement concentrated in the bottom quintile would require only
\result{RequiredBottomQuintile} percent rather than \result{RequiredTopQuintile} percent at the
top. Even that lower requirement is above the assembled rate on either jurisdiction.

\subsection{Pass-through, and what it can do to the comparison}

The condition in Definition~\ref{def:condition} carries a coefficient that the headline comparison
sets to one. Written as $\tau_k \ge \tau_\ell (1 - R)$, it assumes that every dollar of displaced
wages reappears as a dollar of taxable US capital income. Making that explicit,
\begin{equation}
\tau_k \, g \;\ge\; \tau_\ell\,(1 - R), \qquad
\tau_k^{\ast} = \frac{\tau_\ell\,(1 - R)}{g},
\label{eq:passthrough}
\end{equation}
with $g$ the taxable US capital income generated per dollar of displaced wages, bounded in
$[0,1]$ with output preserved. Everything reported above is the case $g = 1$, and it stays the
headline because $g$ cannot be sourced. Incomplete pass-through can only widen the gap on the
marginal rate, never close it: any $g$ below one raises the required rate, and the decomposition of a displaced
wage dollar in Appendix~\ref{app:detail}, which puts $g$ at \result{GLow} to \result{GHigh}, is
scenario arithmetic reported there for that reason.

\subsection{The boundary, and what would have to happen for the condition to close}

The bound $g \le 1$ belongs to the output-preserved case. If AI raises output, a displaced wage
dollar can be associated with more than a dollar of taxable capital income, and $g$ can exceed
one. Stating the condition that way turns a comparison of point estimates into a boundary, which
is the more useful object: Figure~\ref{fig:boundary} draws the fiscal balance
$\tau_k \, g - \tau_\ell (1 - R)$ over the retained wage share and $g$, with the zero contour for
each jurisdiction and each labor tax reading.

Two things can be read straight off it. At the measured retained wage share, the condition closes
only if $g$ reaches \result{GStarFedEasy} to \result{GStarFedHard} on the federal-only version,
or \result{GStarAllEasy} to \result{GStarAllHard} on the all-government one. Every one of those
is above the output-preserved ceiling, so with output preserved the condition does not close at
the assembled marginal rate anywhere on the grid. On the annual rate of
Section~\ref{sec:annual} it closes at every corner, so this boundary is a property of the
marginal object. And the conclusion reverses only in the upper region:
either output rises enough to carry $g$ well past one, or the share of displaced wages that comes
back as wages rises far above what the displaced worker data show.

\subsection{What the rate is a statement about, and the annual counterpart}
\label{sec:annual}

Under full expensing the deduction precedes the income, so the rate assembled above is a
present-value wedge over the life of one marginal investment. The replacement question asks
about tax collected in a year. An earlier version of this paper asserted that stationarity made
the two comparable and left it there. That is not enough, and working out the mapping changes
the answer, so we set it out and report what it gives.

\paragraph{The mapping.} Let a sector hold capital $K$ depreciating at $\delta$, earning gross
capital income $(r+\delta)K$, so net capital income is $rK$. In a stationary state replacement
investment is $I=\delta K$. The annual entity deduction under full expensing is $I=\delta K$; the
annual deduction under economic depreciation is also $\delta K$. \textbf{In steady state the two
coincide}, and the annual entity base is $(r+\delta)K-\delta K = rK$: the whole of net capital
income, normal return and rent alike.

The marginal wedge is a different object. For one marginal project, expensing deducts the outlay
at once and the present value of that deduction offsets the present value of entity tax on the
project's normal return. That is the result of \citet{AcemogluManeraRestrepo2020} which the
assembly above uses, and it is correct on the margin. It is false for the annual flow from
capital already in place, because no deduction is taken this year against last decade's
investment beyond replacement. A zero marginal wedge is therefore consistent with positive
annual receipts, which is the distinction the assembled rate elides.

Carrying that through the layers: equity-financed net capital income bears the entity rate and
then the shareholder layer on what is left; debt-financed net income bears no annual entity tax,
because the interest deduction offsets the interest income, so only the bondholder layer
collects. Hence
\begin{equation}
\tau_k^{\text{annual}} \;=\; (1-d)\bigl[\tau_e + (1-\tau_e)\,\tau_s\bigr] \;+\; d\,\tau_b ,
\label{eq:annual}
\end{equation}
with $d$ the debt share, $\tau_e$ the blended entity rate, $\tau_s$ the shareholder layer and
$\tau_b$ the bondholder rate. Two features matter. The marginal object's debt term is
$\tau_b-\tau_c$, which is negative: that is a subsidy to a marginal debt-financed project, not
negative annual revenue, and it does not belong here. And the rent share $\sigma$ \textbf{does
not appear}, because the annual entity tax reaches normal return and rent alike. The two rent
readings that drive the marginal rate's whole spread collapse to one number.

\paragraph{The shareholder layer, decomposed by holder and treatment.} The layer above is
assembled as $\theta\times$rate$\times$deferral on a taxable share of \result{ThetaTaxable}
percent, which treats everything else as outside the base and applies one deferral factor to
dividends and gains alike. Decomposing it by holder, on the shares of
\citet{RosenthalMucciolo2024}, separates three treatments the single figure conflates.

\result{TradRetireShare} percent of US equity sits in traditional retirement arrangements.
A deductible contribution grown at $r$ and taxed on withdrawal at the same rate leaves the
return untaxed, because the government is a pro-rata co-investor, so the marginal rate on the
inside build-up is zero and treating these holdings as outside the base is correct \emph{on the
margin}. It is wrong for annual revenue: in a mature system those distributions are taxed at
ordinary rates every year. This is the expensing problem of Equation~\ref{eq:annual} appearing
again one layer up, with the same resolution. Roth arrangements, at
\result{RothShare} percent, are untaxed on both objects, having been taxed on contribution.
\result{ForeignEquityShare} percent is held abroad and bears US withholding on dividends,
statutorily \result{WithholdStatutory} percent and \result{WithholdTreatyStd} percent under the
standard portfolio article of the major treaties, though not on portfolio gains. Section~5 sizes
that effect on its own; here it is folded into the layer and taken together with the dividend
split, which is the joint treatment rather than the one-at-a-time version.
\textbf{Only \result{NeverTaxedShare} percent of US corporate equity is never reached by any
federal tax at all}, being nonprofits, government plans and insurance separate accounts.

\paragraph{The retirement term needs a flow derivation, and it is an upper bound.} A holder
share times a distribution rate does not by itself give annual receipts per dollar of current
capital income, so here is the flow. Let the accounts hold stock $A$ earning $rA$, take
contributions $C$ and pay withdrawals $W$ in the year. Deductible contributions cost revenue
$\tau_{\text{ord}}C$ and taxable withdrawals raise $\tau_{\text{ord}}W$, so net annual receipts
are $\tau_{\text{ord}}(W-C)$. If the accounts are stationary in aggregate then $W = C + rA$ and
net receipts are exactly $\tau_{\text{ord}}rA$, which is $\tau_{\text{ord}}$ per dollar of capital
income inside them. That is the condition under which a share times a rate is the right
calculation, and it is what we assume.

It does not hold here, and the direction runs against this paper. US retirement balances have
been accumulating, so $C$ is large relative to $W$, $W-C < rA$, and
$\tau_{\text{ord}}$ is an upper bound rather than the value. Bounding the gap needs aggregate
contribution and withdrawal flows we have not assembled, and we do not claim a figure for it.
What we can show is that the conclusion does not turn on it. Setting the retirement term to
zero, the extreme case of a system that collects nothing, leaves the annual rate at
\result{TauAnnRetZeroFed} percent federally and \result{TauAnnRetZeroAllGov} percent on all government, which
\result{RetZeroClearsFed} and \result{RetZeroClearsAllGov} the harder requirement on each basis. The term
moves the number and not the answer.

The layer rises from \result{LayerPublished} percent as published to
\result{LayerMarginalDecomp} percent on the marginal object, of which
\result{LayerForeignPart} points is foreign withholding, and to \result{LayerAnnualDecomp}
percent on the annual object, the further \result{LayerTradPart} points being retirement
distributions.

Four inputs to the layer were swept in an earlier draft and two are now sourced. The dividend
payout share is net corporate dividend payments over corporate profits after tax in the national
accounts, \result{PayoutCentral} on the ten-year window, which is well above the judgement
interval it replaces and raises the taxable-account part because dividends are taxed on receipt
with no deferral benefit. The effective withholding rate on dividends paid abroad comes from the
Internal Revenue Service data on foreign recipients of US income: of \result{SoiDividendsBn} billion
dollars of dividends paid to foreign persons in 2019, \result{SoiDivSubjectPct} percent was subject
to withholding and that part was taxed at \result{SoiRateOnTaxed} percent, giving an effective rate on
all dividends paid abroad of \result{SoiRateAllDiv} percent. The derivation reproduces the published
total: tax on dividends is \result{SoiDivShareOfWithholding} percent of all withholding, which is what
the source reports. Both endpoints of the range are published figures rather than judgements.
The two that remain swept are the ordinary rate applying to retirement distributions and the Roth
share of defined contribution assets, for the reasons at L4. Sourcing the first two moved the
layer by \result{LayerSourcingMove} points and narrowed every range below.

\paragraph{What it gives, and it goes against this paper.} Each rate is compared with the
requirement computed on its own jurisdictional basis, which a first pass of this exercise failed
to do.

On the \textbf{annual} object the condition closes. Federally the rate is
\result{TauAnnFedDec} percent against the \result{RequiredFedEasy} to \result{RequiredFedHard}
percent required, a margin of \result{AnnMarginFed} points over the harder reading, and it
clears that reading at every corner of the shareholder box, at $g = 1$, whose range is
\result{TauAnnFedLo} to \result{TauAnnFedHi} percent. On all government the rate is
\result{TauAnnAllGovDec} percent against \result{RequiredAllGovEasy} to
\result{RequiredAllGovHard} percent, and it clears at every corner there too.

\paragraph{What "at every corner" does and does not mean.} It means every corner of the
twelve-component rate box, holding the pass-through coefficient at one. It is not a statement
over the pass-through range, and the condition is $\tau_k g \ge \tau_\ell(1-R)$, so the annual
result carries its own boundary: $g^{\ast} = \result{GStarAnnFedEasy}$ on the easier federal
reading and $\result{GStarAnnFedHard}$ on the harder one, running \result{GStarAnnFedBoxLo} to \result{GStarAnnFedBoxHi}
across the shareholder box. The difference from the marginal reading is that these are \emph{below}
one, so the annual condition closes without requiring output to rise, whereas the marginal
boundary of \result{GStarFedEasy} to \result{GStarFedHard} is above the output-preserved ceiling and
therefore does. For pass-through below \result{GStarAnnFedHard} the annual condition fails on the
harder reading too, and we do not claim replacement over the whole pass-through range.

On the \textbf{marginal} object the shortfall survives in sign and not in size. With the
shareholder corrections applied jointly the federal rate is \result{TauMargFedDec} percent
against \result{RequiredFedEasy} percent on the easier reading, a gap of
\result{MargGapFed} points, and the range over the shareholder box,
\result{TauMargFedLo} to \result{TauMargFedHi} percent, straddles the requirement. On all
government the gap is \result{MargGapAllGov} points and the range straddles likewise.

\textbf{We therefore do not report a revenue shortfall.} It closes on the annual object by about
ten points and is indistinguishable from zero on the marginal one. We also do not claim the two rates bracket a
transition path. Both are stationary objects: Equation~\ref{eq:annual} assumes a steady state,
and a steady-state comparison establishes nothing about what is collected while a capital stock
is still building. The direction is intuitive, since during an investment boom current
deductions exceed tax on income from a smaller earlier stock, but an intuition is not a bound
and we do not model the path. Which comparator is right depends on the horizon over which
displaced wage receipts must be replaced, which is a question about the speed of displacement
that this paper does not settle. The headline of the earlier version of this work, that the capital tax falls short of
what is lost on wages, should be read as conditional on the marginal reading and on a
shareholder layer that omitted withholding, and not as a finding about annual revenue.

\subsection{Why the rate is low, and which lever binds on each object}
\label{sec:levers}

The constraint that binds the shareholder layer is who owns the claims. About \result{ThetaUntaxable} percent
of US corporate equity sits outside taxable accounts, leaving \result{ThetaTaxable} percent in them.
That headline figure should be read with the decomposition of
Section~\ref{sec:annual}, which splits it into holdings untaxed on the margin but taxed
annually on distribution, holdings bearing withholding, and the \result{NeverTaxedShare} percent that no
federal tax reaches at all. Taking it as a share beyond reach overstates the case, and the
decomposition is why this section no longer carries the paper. On the sourced range the taxable
share runs \result{ThetaTaxableLow} to \result{ThetaTaxableHigh} percent across a sourced range of \result{ThetaTaxableLow} to \result{ThetaTaxableHigh} percent
\citep{RosenthalAustin2016,RosenthalBurke2020,RosenthalMucciolo2024}. Of the gains that do accrue
there, \result{GainsUntaxedAtDeath} percent are held until death and never taxed, on 2014 data
\citep{CBO2014}, and what is realized is realized late, deferral cutting the present value by a
factor of \result{DeferralLow} to \result{DeferralHigh} on the later CRS estimates
\citep{Gravelle2022}. The two vintages are a decade apart and agree that roughly half of accrued
gains never bear tax, which is why we carry both rather than the newer alone. The debt side has the same
structure: \result{InterestSheltered} percent of corporate interest goes to holders who do not pay
tax on it and \result{BondsRestOfWorld} percent of corporate bonds are held abroad. Together these
are why the owner's layer adds only \result{ShareholderLayerPct} percent to the rate on rents.

Profit shifting is real. How much it matters is a different question from how uncertain it is,
and the two are easy to conflate.

\paragraph{Sensitivity is not importance, and the lever table measures sensitivity.}
Table~\ref{tab:levers} moves each component across its range with the others held at central
values. That reports how much the answer would move if we were wrong about a parameter. It does
not report how much that channel reduces the rate. A parameter pinned to a narrow range by good
evidence has a small swing and may still be the largest determinant of the level, which is
exactly the argument Appendix~\ref{app:detail} already makes about the taxable-holder share. The
same logic applies to every row, so the table is reported here as an uncertainty diagnostic and
no claim about relative importance is drawn from it.

\paragraph{The counterfactual comparison, by mechanism rather than by package.} To ask which
channel matters we set each to its no-friction value and measure what happens to the level.
Ownership is not one mechanism but two, the share of equity held in taxable accounts and the
deferral and step-up that erode what reaches those accounts, and bundling them would overstate
the comparison. They are kept apart.

The alone column understates the ownership mechanisms because they are complements: fixing
deferral makes the taxable base matter more and the reverse. The Shapley attribution, which
averages over every order of removal and sums exactly to the joint effect of
\result{ShJoint} points, puts the taxable base at \result{ShThetaShapley}, deferral at
\result{ShDeferShapley} and profit shifting at \result{ShShiftShapley}.

\paragraph{The ordering holds on one object and reverses on the other.} Everything above is
computed on the marginal wedge, and the comparison does not survive the move to annual revenue.
Sweeping each component across its whole sourced range with the others at central values, profit
shifting moves the marginal rate by \result{ShiftSwingMarginal} and the taxable shareholder base
by \result{ShThetaSwingMarginal}, which is the ordering the section has just argued. On the
annual rate of Equation~\ref{eq:annual} the two are \result{ShiftSwingAnnual} and
\result{ThetaSwingAnnual}, so profit shifting ranks \result{ShiftRankAnnual} of seven and the
shareholder base \result{ThetaRankAnnual}, and the order flips. The reason is mechanical: the
shifted share enters the entity layer, and the entity layer is precisely what the annual object
collects and the marginal object washes out. We therefore do not claim that ownership binds
harder than profit shifting as a general matter. It does so on the marginal wedge, it does not on
annual revenue, and a reader who cares about collected revenue should take the second.

So the comparison the title draws is this one. The single largest mechanism, the taxable
shareholder base, is about \result{ShRatioTheta} times profit shifting. The two ownership
mechanisms together are about \result{ShRatioPair} times it, and that figure is a package and is
labelled as one. What does not hold is a claim that every ownership mechanism individually dwarfs
shifting: deferral does not.

Neither counterfactual is a policy. No reform abolishes profit shifting or places all equity in
taxable hands with no deferral, so the rows bound what each mechanism can contribute rather than
proposing anything.

Under the Karabarbounis and Neiman rent reading profit shifting contributes exactly nothing, and
that follows from the model rather than from the data: shifting operates only on the rent
component and that reading sets the rent share to zero. It is reported for completeness and
carries no evidential weight in the comparison.

\paragraph{The denominator caveat, which runs against us.} Both sources for the shifted share
measure a share of \emph{foreign} profits, while the assembly applies the parameter to the whole
rent base, treating a dollar of US rent as booked abroad with that probability. That overstates
shifting unless essentially all rent is earned through foreign affiliates. Correcting it would
raise the assembled marginal rate and narrow the gap on it, and it would also
\emph{shrink} the profit-shifting counterfactual above, which is the direction that strengthens
the comparison rather than the paper's headline. It is stated at L11 in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} and bounded there.

\subsection{Checks, and what this module does not reach}

Two published figures check the assembly and one limitation runs against our own finding; both are set out in Appendix~\ref{app:detail}. In short, our shareholder layer corroborates the Congressional Research Service's text figure once the two are put on a common base, and the base construction treats foreign holders as bearing no tax at the owner level though dividends to them bear withholding tax. That effect is sized in Section~\ref{sec:fiscal} above: at a fifteen percent treaty rate it raises the assembled rate to \result{TauKWithholding} percent, and to \result{TauKJointWeakening} percent with the filers' own debt share as well, both below the \result{RequiredAllGovEasy} percent the condition requires. What stays open is the actual effective withholding rate, which varies by treaty and with the split between dividends, which bear it, and capital gains realized by foreign holders, which generally do not.
