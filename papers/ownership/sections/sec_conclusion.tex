\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

If income moves from labor to capital, the tax system has to collect on the second what it loses
on the first. We stated that requirement as a condition, assembled both of its sides from sourced
components, and found that the answer depends on which tax rate is put into it.

On a consistently federal basis, with the shareholder layer decomposed by holder, the effective
marginal wedge on income shifting from wages to capital is \result{CfBaseFed} percent, against
the \result{RequiredFedEasy} to \result{RequiredFedHard} percent replacement requires. Written with an explicit pass-through
coefficient the comparison becomes a boundary, and on that object the condition closes only where
taxable capital income per displaced wage dollar reaches \result{GStarFedEasy} to
\result{GStarFedHard}, above the ceiling of one that output-preserving displacement implies.

A marginal wedge is not a rate of revenue collection, and the mapping between them is not a
detail. The distinction is standard \citep{DevereuxGriffith2003} and is not ours; what we add is
carrying it through an assembled replacement condition. In a stationary state expensing and economic depreciation give the same annual deduction,
so the annual entity base is the whole of net capital income and not rents alone. The annual rate
is \result{TauAnnFedDec} percent centrally and \result{TauAnnFedLo} to \result{TauAnnFedHi}
percent at every corner of the rate box at $g = 1$, so on that object the condition closes
throughout that box and its own pass-through boundary falls to \result{GStarAnnFedHard} on the harder
reading, below the ceiling of one rather than above it,
and the rent share that drives the marginal rate's entire spread drops out of the algebra. The
gap between the objects, \result{AnnualMarginalGapLo} to \result{AnnualMarginalGapHi} points, is
larger than any disagreement among the twelve components. We therefore do not report a revenue
shortfall, and we do not claim that absence as a discovery: \citet{HotteTheodorakopoulosKoutroumpis2024}
reach it from European panel data by a reduced-form route, and our structural and forward-looking
computation on US data agrees with them. We do not claim the two rates bracket a transition path.
Both are stationary objects, and a stationary calculation cannot establish what is collected
while a capital stock is still building. Choosing between them needs the speed of displacement
and a transition we do not model.

The reason the rate is low is the contribution, and it is not mainly profit shifting. Set against
counterfactuals rather than against the width of an uncertainty interval, eliminating profit
shifting entirely raises the marginal rate by \result{CfGainShiftFed} percentage points and leaves
it above the easier federal threshold but below the harder one, while completing the reach of the
shareholder tax raises it by
\result{CfGainOwnFed}. Both are computed on the decomposed layer and both are smaller than the
earlier draft reported, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:fiscal}. The Shapley attribution that separates ownership into its two
mechanisms was computed on the first-pass layer and is not quoted as a result here, because
decomposing the layer changes what the mechanisms mean; only its ordering is claimed. The comparison is not drawn from the sensitivity table, which reports how wrong the
answer could be rather than what each channel costs, and which this paper now labels as such. The
claim is comparative rather than dismissive, and it is specific to the marginal rate: on the
annual rate the ordering reverses, and profit shifting moves that rate more than the shareholder
base does.
About
\result{ThetaUntaxable} percent of US corporate equity sits outside taxable accounts, though only
\result{NoOwnerTaxShare} percent of it bears no owner-level tax once retirement distributions
and dividend withholding are counted, and of the gains that do accrue in taxable accounts,
\result{GainsUntaxedAtDeath} percent are never taxed at all. A legislature can change a rate
more readily than it can change who owns the claims, and the rate operates on a base most
households are not in. That is an observation about feasibility. It is not a demonstration that
no rate would raise enough, and our own condition is satisfied by a large enough one.

That single fact does more work than one result. The same ownership concentration means
household debt-service capacity cannot be restored by broadening ownership, since
\result{HHEquityOutsideHouseholds} percent of corporate equity sits outside the household sector
and broadened ownership without liquidity moves the boundary by exactly zero, which follows from
how we define spendable resources rather than from the data. And through the equity-wealth
channel we measure, a bust transmits weakly to households, because the assets that fall are held
where the propensity to consume is lowest; that is a statement about one channel on two
historical episodes, not a general claim that a bust is a small credit event, and the financing
channels we do not measure could carry more. The revenue question, the incidence question and the transmission
question all turn on the same ownership structure. They are not established to the same
standard, and we do not present them as one result: the first is assembled from sourced
components, the second is arithmetic on measured shares, and the third is an illustration drawn
from two episodes in which an equity fall was not the only thing happening.

We do not claim this settles which tax base is right, and we state the case against our own
choice where it arises. Two components are swept rather than sourced, the pass-through
coefficient cannot be sourced at all, and the household figures are incidence arithmetic rather
than forecasts. What holds on every base and under every reading we can defend is
narrower than the earlier version of this work claimed. No revenue shortfall is established on
either rate. The ownership structure that the shareholder layer rests on is real and is measured
here more finely than before, but it governs the marginal rate rather than the annual one, and on
the annual one the entity layer does the work.
