\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

If income moves from labor to capital, the tax system has to collect on the second what it loses
on the first. We stated that requirement as a condition, assembled both of its sides from sourced
components, and found that the answer depends on which tax rate is put into it.

On a consistently federal basis the effective marginal wedge on income shifting from wages to
capital is \result{TauKFederalOnly} percent, against the \result{RequiredFedEasy} to
\result{RequiredFedHard} percent replacement requires. Written with an explicit pass-through
coefficient the comparison becomes a boundary, and on that object the condition closes only where
taxable capital income per displaced wage dollar reaches \result{GStarFedEasy} to
\result{GStarFedHard}, above the ceiling of one that output-preserving displacement implies.

A marginal wedge is not a rate of revenue collection, and the mapping between them is not a
detail. In a stationary state expensing and economic depreciation give the same annual deduction,
so the annual entity base is the whole of net capital income and not rents alone. The annual rate
is \result{TauAnnualCentral} percent centrally and \result{TauAnnualLo} to \result{TauAnnualHi}
percent at every corner of the parameter box, so on that object the condition closes everywhere,
and the rent share that drives the marginal rate's entire spread drops out of the algebra. The
gap between the objects, \result{AnnualMarginalGapLo} to \result{AnnualMarginalGapHi} points, is
larger than any disagreement among the twelve components. We therefore do not report a revenue
shortfall. The two rates bound a transition, and placing an episode on that path requires the
speed of displacement, which we do not measure.

The reason the rate is low is the contribution, and it is not mainly profit shifting. Set against
counterfactuals rather than against the width of an uncertainty interval, eliminating profit
shifting entirely raises the rate by \result{CfGainShift} percentage points and leaves it short
of the threshold, while completing the reach of the shareholder tax raises it by
\result{CfGainOwn} and clears it. Separating ownership into its two mechanisms and attributing by
Shapley value, the taxable shareholder base contributes \result{ShThetaShapley} points against
\result{ShShiftShapley} for profit shifting, about \result{ShRatioTheta} times as much, while
deferral at \result{ShDeferShapley} is a smaller margin over shifting than the bundled figure
suggests. The comparison is not drawn from the sensitivity table, which reports how wrong the
answer could be rather than what each channel costs, and which this paper now labels as such. The
claim is comparative rather than dismissive: profit shifting is real and costly, and it is not
where most of the shortfall lives.
About
\result{ThetaUntaxable} percent of US corporate equity sits in forms the individual income tax
barely reaches, and of the gains that do accrue in taxable accounts,
\result{GainsUntaxedAtDeath} percent are never taxed at all. A legislature can change a rate
more readily than it can change who owns the claims, and the rate operates on a base most
households are not in. That is an observation about feasibility. It is not a demonstration that
no rate would raise enough, and our own condition is satisfied by a large enough one.

That single fact does more work than one result. The same ownership concentration means
household debt-service capacity cannot be restored by broadening ownership, since
\result{HHEquityOutsideHouseholds} percent of corporate equity sits outside the household sector
and broadened ownership without liquidity moves the boundary by exactly zero, which follows from
how we define spendable resources rather than from the data. And through the equity-wealth
channel we measure, a bust transmits weakly to households, because the assets that fall are held
where the propensity to consume is lowest; that is a statement about one channel on two
historical episodes, not a general claim that a bust is a small credit event, and the financing
channels we do not measure could carry more. The revenue problem, the incidence problem and the
transmission result are three readings of one structural fact.

We do not claim this settles which tax base is right, and we state the case against our own
choice where it arises. Two components are swept rather than sourced, the pass-through
coefficient cannot be sourced at all, and the household figures are incidence arithmetic rather
than forecasts. What holds on every base and under every reading we can defend is that the
central case falls short, that the shortfall is governed by ownership rather than by enforcement,
and that the instruments which would actually move it are the ones that have had the least
attention.
