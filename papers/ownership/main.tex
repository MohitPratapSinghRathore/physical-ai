\begin{abstract}
\noindent
If income shifts from labor to capital, can the tax system collect on the capital what it loses
on the wages? We state the condition that has to hold and assemble both of its sides from sourced
components. On a consistently federal basis the effective marginal rate on income moving from
wages to capital is \result{TauKFederalOnly} percent against the \result{RequiredFedEasy} to
\result{RequiredFedHard} percent replacement requires. Writing the condition with an explicit
pass-through coefficient turns a comparison of point estimates into a boundary: it closes only
where taxable capital income per displaced wage dollar reaches \result{GStarFedEasy} to
\result{GStarFedHard}, above the ceiling of one that output-preserving displacement implies, so
it closes only if output rises substantially. The tightest constraint is not where the literature
has looked. Once its share is sourced from two agreeing studies rather than swept over a
judgement range, profit shifting ranks last of seven levers. What binds hardest is ownership:
about
\result{ThetaUntaxable} percent of US corporate equity sits in forms the individual income tax
barely reaches. The same concentration governs two further results measured here. Household
debt-service capacity cannot be restored by broadening ownership, because
\result{HHEquityOutsideHouseholds} percent of corporate equity sits outside the household sector
and ownership without liquidity moves the boundary by exactly zero. And an equity bust transmits
weakly to households, because the assets that fall are held where the propensity to consume is
lowest. One fact, three consequences, and a shortfall a change in the rate alone does not close.
\end{abstract}
