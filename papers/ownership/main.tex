\begin{abstract}
\noindent
If income shifts from labor to capital, can the tax system collect on the capital what it loses
on the wages? A widely drawn conclusion says no. It does not survive careful construction of the
tax rate. We state the condition, assemble both sides from sourced components, then map the
marginal wedge on a new investment to the rate revenue is collected at in a year. Which rate is used decides the answer, on the standard marginal versus average distinction
\citep{DevereuxGriffith2003}: in a stationary state expensing and economic depreciation give the
same annual deduction, so the annual base is all of net capital income and the rent share driving
the marginal rate's spread drops out. Federally the annual rate is \result{TauAnnFedDec} percent
against the \result{RequiredFedEasy} to \result{RequiredFedHard} percent required, clearing the
harder reading at every corner, while the marginal rate is \result{TauMargFedDec} percent, short
by \result{MargGapFed} points with a range that straddles. No shortfall is established on either
rate, corroborating \citet{HotteTheodorakopoulosKoutroumpis2024} by an independent route. The annual figure is a
steady-state scenario, not a forecast: it assumes a stationary capital stock and constant rates,
it says nothing about collections during a transition, and two of its shareholder inputs are
swept rather than sourced. Decomposing that layer also revises the ownership figure the argument rests
on: \result{TradRetireShare} percent of US equity sits in traditional arrangements taxed on
distribution, and only \result{NoOwnerTaxShare} percent bears no owner-level federal tax, those
holdings still bearing the entity tax.
\end{abstract}
