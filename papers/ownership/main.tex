\begin{abstract}
\noindent
If income shifts from labor to capital, can the tax system collect on the capital what it loses
on the wages? The answer depends on which tax rate is measured, and the two standard objects give
opposite answers. We state the replacement condition, assemble both sides from sourced
components, then derive the mapping between the marginal wedge on a new investment and the rate
revenue is collected at in a year. On the marginal object the rate is
\result{TauMarginalKN} to \result{TauMarginalBarkai} percent against the
\result{RequiredFedEasy} to \result{RequiredFedHard} percent replacement requires, and the
condition fails. On the annual object it is \result{TauAnnualCentral} percent centrally and
\result{TauAnnualLo} to \result{TauAnnualHi} percent at every corner of the parameter box, and
the condition closes everywhere. The reason is structural: in a stationary state expensing and
economic depreciation give the same annual deduction, so the annual entity base is all of net
capital income rather than rents alone, and the rent share that drives the marginal rate's whole
spread drops out. The gap, \result{AnnualMarginalGapLo} to \result{AnnualMarginalGapHi} points,
exceeds every disagreement among the components. Which lever
binds also flips: the taxable shareholder base outranks profit shifting on the marginal object
and the ordering reverses on the annual one. We report the two rates as bounds on a transition
rather than a shortfall, and show that the ownership concentration behind the shareholder layer,
about \result{ThetaUntaxable} percent of US corporate equity in forms the income tax barely
reaches, still governs the household side whichever object is chosen.
\end{abstract}
