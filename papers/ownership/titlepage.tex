\noindent\textbf{This is the second of two companion papers.} The first, \textit{Labor Backing
Accounts: Who Holds the Claims That Wages Pay?}, reports the measurement this paper draws on:
what income services the US claim stock and who holds, guarantees or owes it. This paper asks
whether the tax system can replace the revenue when income moves from labor to capital. Each
stands alone and neither repeats the other.

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent\textbf{On the title, which has been weakened deliberately.} An earlier draft read
\textit{Ownership, Not Profit Shifting: Why Capital Taxation Cannot Replace Wage Taxes}. Both
halves claimed more than the paper shows. ``Cannot replace'' contradicts the paper's own
boundary, which closes if output rises enough per displaced wage dollar, so the title now says
the rate falls short. ``Not profit shifting'' rested on a lever ranking that is close: profit
shifting ranks third, and widening its single-source range by a little moves it to first, as
Section 5 now reports. The comparative claim is what survives. Alternatives: (1) \textit{Who Owns
the Capital? The Binding Constraint on Replacing a Wage Tax}; (2) \textit{Ownership More Than
Profit Shifting: The Limits of Capital Taxation}.
